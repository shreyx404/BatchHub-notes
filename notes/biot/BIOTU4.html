Module 01 · Orientation

Foundations: Security Goals & the CIA Triad

Before the challenges, threats and countermeasures — the vocabulary. Every topic in this unit ultimately defends one of three goals.

Definition

IoT security is the practice of protecting connected devices, the networks they use, and the data they exchange from unauthorized access, manipulation and disruption.

Syllabus area (Unit IV)Covered in
Challenges and threats to IoT securityModules 02, 04 & 05
Encryption and authentication techniquesModules 07 & 08
Privacy concerns and regulationsModule 09
Best practices for securing IoT devicesModules 03, 06 & 10

1.1The CIA triad — your answer-writing framework

Classical information security is organised around three goals. Learn to map every mechanism and every attack onto them — it turns scattered facts into structured answers.

  • Confidentiality — data can only be read by authorised parties. Attacked by: eavesdropping, unsecured networks, data exposure, lack of encryption. Defended by: encryption (Module 07).
  • Integrity — data is not altered in transit or storage. Attacked by: tampering, spoofing, manipulated commands. Defended by: TLS integrity checks, digital signatures.
  • Availability — systems remain usable when needed. Attacked by: botnet DDoS floods and ransomware that locks devices. Defended by: monitoring, updates, segmentation.
Core vocabulary

Vulnerability — a weakness (e.g., a default password). Threat — a potential attack that exploits a vulnerability (e.g., brute-force). Attack surface — the sum of all points where an attacker could attempt entry. Risk — the likelihood and impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability.

Exam tip

Whenever a question asks “how does X improve IoT security?”, structure the answer around the CIA triad. Example — TLS: encryption → confidentiality, certificates → authenticity, tamper detection → integrity. Examiners reward the framework.

Likely exam question

Q. Define IoT security and state the three classic security goals. [2–3 marks]

Module 02 · Syllabus — challenges

Key Challenges

Five structural reasons why IoT security is difficult — a favourite “list the key challenges of IoT security” question.

1

Limited Resources

Many IoT devices have constrained processing power and memory, making it difficult to implement robust security features.

Consequence: heavy cryptography gets sacrificed for cost and battery life → this is exactly why lightweight cryptography exists (Module 07.4).

2

Unsecured Networks

Devices often connect through unsecured networks, exposing them to eavesdropping and data interception.

Consequence: traffic crossing Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Zigbee or public hotspots can be read in transit (man-in-the-middle).

3

Lack of Basic Security Features

Some devices lack fundamental security features like encryption and strong authentication, leaving them vulnerable.

Consequence: time-to-market and cost pressure mean devices ship “insecure by default”.

4

Data Exposure

Unencrypted data in transit or at rest can be easily intercepted and sold, leading to privacy violations.

Consequence: IoT collects intimate data — location, health, home-activity patterns — highly valuable to attackers.

5

Vast Attack Surface

Billions of connected devices offer a massive, easily accessible attack surface for malicious actors to exploit.

Consequence: the attacker only needs one weak device to reach an entire network.

Memory hook — “BREAD”

Basic security missing · Resources limited · Exposure of data · Attack surface vast · Devices on unsecured networks.

Likely exam question

Q. List and briefly explain any five key challenges of IoT security. [5 marks]

Module 03 · Syllabus — solutions & best practices

Key Solutions: The Security Toolkit

The seven countermeasures that answer the five challenges above — a favourite “list the key solutions” question.

1

Strong Authentication

Implement strong, unique passwords for devices and networks, and use multifactor authentication (MFA) where available.

Defeats: brute-force attacks and default-credential takeovers.

2

Data Encryption

Encrypt all data, both in transit and at rest, using secure protocols like TLS/HTTPS.

Defeats: eavesdropping and data exposure.

3

Secure Network Development

Create secure network environments and segment networks to limit the potential impact of a breach.

Effect: one compromised device stays contained in its segment.

4

Regular Updates & Patch Management

Keep all device firmware and software updated to patch vulnerabilities that could be exploited.

Defeats: known-vulnerability attacks on outdated devices.

5

Continuous Monitoring

Continuously monitor devices and networks for unusual activity to detect and address potential threats promptly.

Effect: early detection — small problems never become incidents.

6

Secure Device Configuration

Disable unused features (like Bluetooth) and change default settings to reduce the attack surface.

Effect: fewer open doors, fewer entry points.

7

Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)

Utilise PKI and digital certificates to simplify and secure the encryption and decryption of data for transactions.

Effect: scalable cryptographic identity for millions of devices.

Module 04 · Syllabus — challenges & threats · Part 1

Security Challenges: The Full Landscape

The complete catalogue — nine core challenges, then the extended landscape grouped by device, data, network, human and adversary level. The names alone are prime short-question material.

4.1The nine core challenges

  1. Weak Authentication — many IoT devices use default or easily guessable passwords, making them vulnerable to unauthorized access through brute-force attacks.
  2. Lack of Data Encryption — critical and personal data transmitted by IoT devices is often unencrypted, making it vulnerable to interception and theft.
  3. Vulnerable Firmware and Software — short development cycles and low costs lead to insufficient security testing.
  4. Insecure Communications — devices and networks can lack proper security protocols, exposing data and credentials to attackers.
  5. Maintenance and Patching Difficulties — devices may have limited ability to be updated, leaving vulnerabilities unpatched for extended periods.
  6. Expanding Attack Surface — the sheer scale and diversity of devices means each device is a potential entry point.
  7. Supply Chain Vulnerabilities — flaws introduced during the design or manufacturing of devices and their components.
  8. Physical Security Gaps — devices can be targets for tampering, theft or damage, leading to data loss or system downtime.
  9. Lack of Visibility and Management — organisations struggle to identify, track and manage all connected devices → “shadow IoT” (unauthorized devices) and increased risk.

4.2Device-level challenges

  • Weak passwords — devices ship with default passwords that are too simple or widely known; users often overlook changing them, leaving devices open to unauthorized access.
  • Insecure interfaces — IoT devices interface with users through web or mobile applications; poor authentication on these interfaces leads to unauthorized access and control, data breaches, privacy invasion and device manipulation.
  • Use of insecure or outdated components — hardware, software or firmware that is outdated or no longer receives security updates opens the device — and the entire network — to attackers.
  • Limited device management — without management systems to monitor, update and secure devices, they fall further behind against new security threats.
  • Lack of a secure update mechanism — updates (for security, features, functionality) must be provided by the owner; without them devices remain susceptible. The update channel itself must also be secure — downloading updates securely.
  • Physical safety risks — a major concern in manufacturing, healthcare and home automation: if devices are hacked or malfunction, unauthorized control of industrial machinery can cause accidents, and a compromised medical device can cause direct physical harm.

4.3Data-level challenges

  • Lack of encryption (recap) — data sent from or to a device can be easily intercepted and read when secure encrypted protocols are not used → data theft and privacy breaches.
  • Insecure data transfer and storage — data sent over unencrypted channels or stored with inadequate protection leads to breaches exposing personal and confidential information.
  • Data privacy — if sensitive data is not properly protected it can be intercepted or accessed by unauthorized parties; unsecured transmission/storage and physical attacks also damage data integrity.
  • Insufficient privacy protection — devices without strong privacy mechanisms are susceptible to breaches exposing private information; the problem grows when a fleet mixes devices with different levels of protection.
  • Data leaks from IoT systems — sensitive data seeping out through misconfigured devices, services or APIs.

4.4Network & protocol-level challenges

  • Insecure communications (recap) — protocols and channels without proper security expose data and credentials in transit.
  • 5G vulnerabilities — 5G increases the speed of communication, but its increased speed and connectivity also increase the attack surface of IoT systems, making them targets for large-scale attacks.
  • DNS threats — the Domain Name System is required for IoT connectivity: it connects IoT devices to remote servers and services. DNS threats manipulate queries and responses to redirect devices to malicious sites → compromise of sensitive data, spread of malware and hijacking of devices.

DNS vulnerabilities that enable attacks:

  • Incorrect DNS records — typos and altered records that misdirect traffic.
  • Non-resolvable domains — incorrectly configured hosts or default settings.
  • Old, outdated or unsupported software and hardware.
  • Open DNS resolvers that can be exploited for DoS attacks.
  • Weak security settings — improperly configured Access Control Lists (ACLs), unapplied security patches, and other misconfigurations.

4.5Human & organisational challenges

  • Shadow IoT — unauthorized IoT devices connected to a network; often smart devices brought in by trusted employees. They are a security risk because they bypass standard security measures.
  • IoT security awareness — users unaware of IoT security overlook secure configuration, neglect firmware updates and use weak passwords; training and enforced best practices reduce user-related risks.
  • Lack of compliance — security standards and regulations exist to ensure secure, reliable IoT systems; organisations that don’t comply leave devices and networks vulnerable — and may face legal consequences if a data breach results from substandard security.
  • Espionage and eavesdropping — IoT devices have advanced sensors and recording capabilities; once hacked they become easy tools for monitoring personal activities — a serious threat exposing confidential and critical information.
  • IoT physical security — devices are susceptible to tampering, theft or destruction → unauthorized access, data loss or unplanned downtime. Tamper-proof enclosures and surveillance cameras reduce these risks.

4.6Adversary-level challenges

  • Botnets — connected devices attacked by malicious software; remotely controlled to carry out malicious activities such as stealing data. IoT devices without requisite security protocols are vulnerable to becoming part of a botnet. → Deep dive in Module 05, FIG. 07.
  • Ransomware — malicious software that encrypts data; hackers threaten to publish the data or block access unless a ransom is paid. IoT devices are attractive targets due to the sensitive data they collect and store. → Deep dive in Module 05, FIG. 08.
  • Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) — complex, sophisticated attacks that enter IoT systems and remain hidden for long periods, collecting sensitive data over time and moving laterally to compromise other devices and systems.
  • Security threats (general) — malware attacks, phishing and unauthorized access increasingly target IoT; cybercriminals use IoT devices to gain access to networks, disrupting normal functioning and affecting sensitive information.
  • Weak authentication & authorization — weak methods make unauthorized access easy, leading to data breaches and larger network intrusions.
Deep dive — the “demon process” example from Linux

In Linux, several regular low-priority jobs need to be executed regularly; these run as daemons (background processes). APT malware can disguise itself as — or operate like — such a routine “demon process” in the kernel, hiding in plain sight. Once embedded in the IoT system, it collects confidential information slowly over time and moves laterally, compromising other devices and systems while evading notice.

Exam tip

For a 10-mark “security challenges of IoT” answer, name and explain 8–10 challenges drawn from different groups (device / data / network / human / adversary). Grouping proves understanding and prevents you from listing five variations of the same weakness.

Module 05 · Syllabus — challenges & threats · Part 2

Security Threats: Anatomy of Attacks

Challenges are weaknesses; threats are what attackers actually do with them. These five are the exam’s favourite threats.

Definition

A vulnerability is a weakness; a threat is the potential attack that exploits it; the attack surface is every place an attack could be attempted.

5.1Ransomware & malware

Insecure devices can be infected with malware that disrupts functionality, steals data, or enables further attacks. Ransomware is the most notorious form: it encrypts data and demands payment — hackers threaten to publish the data or block access unless a ransom is paid. IoT devices are attractive targets because of the sensitive data they collect and store, and because many cannot be easily restored, patched or cleaned.

In the news — real-world wake-up calls
  • Government computers attacked with ransomware in 2017.
  • India tops the global malware attack chart as AI drives a ransomware surge.
  • New ransomware “Yurei” adopts open-source tools for double-extortion campaigns.
  • EU agency confirms ransomware attack behind airport disruptions.
  • Jaguar Land Rover production still idled as cyberattacks spread across the auto industry.
  • IoT and ransomware are big security risks — and health systems feel unprepared.

5.2Botnets & DDoS

A botnet is a network of compromised devices (“bots”) infected with malicious software and remotely controlled by an attacker (the botmaster) through a command-and-control (C&C) server. IoT devices that lack the requisite security protocols — especially those still on default credentials — are routinely conscripted. Botnets carry out malicious activities such as stealing data and launching large-scale Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks that flood victims with traffic and knock services offline — a direct attack on availability.

Good to know — Mirai, the textbook botnet

The infamous Mirai botnet conscripted hundreds of thousands of IoT devices (cameras, routers, recorders) by simply trying a short list of factory-default usernames and passwords — a perfect illustration of Modules 02, 04 and 05 connecting: weak authentication (challenge) → botnet conscription (threat) → record-scale DDoS (impact).

5.3Data breaches & theft

Attackers gain access to sensitive personal or corporate data collected by IoT devices due to weak security — health records, location histories, home-occupancy patterns, industrial metrics. Consequences: identity theft, financial fraud, privacy violations and regulatory fines.

5.4Unauthorized access & control

Hackers can gain control of IoT devices, leading to misuse: spying through security cameras, or manipulating critical systems — thermostats, locks, medical devices, industrial actuators. This links directly to the physical-safety challenge (Module 04.2).

5.5Network intrusion

A compromised IoT device can serve as an entry point (beachhead) for attackers to access an entire corporate or home network — and then move laterally to high-value systems. This is precisely why segmentation (Module 06) matters.

Likely exam question

Q. Explain any five security threats in IoT with examples. [5 marks]

Module 06 · Addressing IoT security requirements

Learn → Segment → Protect

The three-phase enterprise framework for addressing IoT security requirements — a guaranteed short-question topic.

  1. LEARN — build visibility. With complete network visibility, security solutions authenticate and classify IoT devices, build a risk profile for each, and assign them to IoT device groups. Principle: you cannot protect what you cannot see.
  2. SEGMENT — contain by policy. Once the enterprise understands its IoT attack surface, devices are segmented into policy-driven groups based on their risk profiles. A breached smart camera cannot then reach the database server.
  3. PROTECT — enforce everywhere. Policy-driven IoT groups and internal network segmentation enable monitoring, inspection and policy enforcement based on activity at various points within the infrastructure.
Exam tip — 3-mark ready-made

Learn = visibility & classification into device groups · Segment = policy-driven grouping by risk profile · Protect = monitor, inspect, enforce across the infrastructure.

Module 07 · Syllabus — encryption techniques

Encryption Techniques

Symmetric, asymmetric, TLS and lightweight cryptography — how IoT keeps data unreadable to everyone except its intended recipient.

Definition

Encryption converts data into an unreadable format (ciphertext) to prevent unauthorized access.

  • Symmetric encryption — a single secret key for both encryption and decryption; speed and efficiency. Examples: AES, Blowfish.
  • Asymmetric encryption — a pair of mathematically linked keys (public + private); enhanced security; underpins PKI.
  • Transport Layer Security (TLS/SSL) — a widely used protocol that encrypts data in transit between IoT devices and servers.
  • Lightweight cryptography — designed for resource-constrained IoT devices: strong security with minimal computational power and memory.

7.1Symmetric encryption

Definition

Symmetric encryption uses a single, shared private key for both encrypting and decrypting data — offering speed and efficiency suitable for the large data volumes of IoT networks. Its primary challenge in IoT is securely distributing the key, which is typically solved by using asymmetric encryption to establish a temporary shared symmetric key — a session key — as seen in TLS/SSL.

How symmetric encryption works in IoT:

  1. Shared secret key — both the IoT device and the server hold the same secret key.
  2. Encryption — the device uses the shared key to encrypt outgoing data, transforming it into unreadable ciphertext.
  3. Decryption — the server receives the ciphertext and uses the same shared key to decrypt it back into readable plaintext.
✓ Benefits for IoT
  • Speed and efficiency — symmetric algorithms are generally much faster than asymmetric ones, ideal for encrypting the large amounts of data IoT devices produce.
  • Resource-friendly — less processing power and memory required, crucial for constrained devices.
✕ The central challenge
  • Key distribution — the biggest hurdle: securely delivering and managing the shared secret key among numerous devices.
  • If the key is intercepted during distribution, the entire system is compromised.

Solutions in IoT:

  • Hybrid approaches — IoT systems combine symmetric and asymmetric encryption to overcome the key-distribution problem.
  • TLS/SSL and HTTPS — public-key cryptography establishes the initial secure connection, then a new, temporary symmetric session key is exchanged for the duration of the session. The session key encrypts the bulk of the communication — delivering both security and efficiency.
  • MQTT over TLS — for devices using the MQTT messaging protocol, TLS secures the data stream using this same hybrid approach.

7.2Asymmetric encryption

Definition

Asymmetric (public-key) encryption employs a pair of mathematically linked keys — a public key for encryption and a private key for decryption — enabling secure communication and authentication in IoT: anyone can encrypt to a device, only that device can read it.

How asymmetric encryption works in IoT:

  1. Key generation — each IoT device generates a unique pair: a public key (shareable widely) and a private key (kept secret).
  2. Secure communication — to send a message to a device, another device or server encrypts it using the recipient’s public key.
  3. Decryption — only the recipient, holding the corresponding private key, can decrypt and read the message → confidentiality.

RSA

Rivest–Shamir–Adleman — an early public-key cryptosystem; versatile: used for key exchange, digital signatures and certificates in IoT.

ECC

Elliptic Curve Cryptography — a modern approach offering security similar to RSA with much smaller key sizes, ideal for the limited processing power and memory of IoT devices.

Diffie–Hellman

A specific asymmetric technique used to securely establish a shared symmetric key between devices without exchanging the key itself.

Applications in IoT security:

  • Secure key exchange — establishes secure channels and exchanges the symmetric keys needed for faster bulk-data encryption without compromising security.
  • Digital signatures — the sender’s private key creates a signature verified with the sender’s public key: proves the sender’s identity (authentication) and confirms the message hasn’t been tampered with (data integrity).
  • Entity authentication — devices use digital certificates containing their public keys to authenticate themselves to servers or other devices, verifying identity and ensuring communication with the legitimate party.

7.3Transport Layer Security (TLS)

Definition

TLS — the successor to SSL — establishes encrypted, authenticated, and data-integrity-protected communication channels between devices and cloud platforms. It ensures the confidentiality and integrity of data in transit, preventing eavesdropping and tampering, and is a fundamental requirement for many IoT platforms for device-to-cloud communication.

How TLS secures IoT communications:

  • Encryption — TLS uses a combination of symmetric and public-key encryption to scramble data, making it unreadable to anyone who intercepts it in transmission.
  • Authentication — digital certificates verify the identities of both the IoT device and the server, ensuring each is communicating with the legitimate party.
  • Data integrity — TLS incorporates measures to detect if data has been tampered with or altered in transit, ensuring the data received is the same as the data sent.

Why TLS is essential for IoT:

Vulnerable devices

IoT devices often operate in potentially insecure environments and may lack robust built-in security features — making their communications a prime target.

Protecting sensitive data

IoT systems handle personal data, health metrics and industrial control data that must be protected from unauthorized access.

Preventing malicious commands

By authenticating the source of commands and encrypting their contents, TLS prevents attackers from sending malicious instructions to devices.

Ensuring platform compliance

Cloud IoT services such as AWS IoT Core mandate TLS for all in-transit device communications with the platform.

7.4Lightweight cryptography (LWC)

Definition

Lightweight cryptography provides essential security for resource-constrained IoT devices by offering computationally efficient, energy-saving, and memory-efficient algorithms — unlike traditional methods that are too demanding for small sensors and wearables. LWC algorithms are optimised for small circuit sizes and low power consumption, protecting confidentiality and integrity without draining batteries or exceeding memory and processing limits.

Why LWC is needed for IoT devices:

  • Resource constraints — smart sensors and wearables have limited power, processing and memory; traditional algorithms such as AES and RSA are too resource-intensive.
  • Battery life — heavy algorithms consume significant energy → rapid battery drain and reduced operational lifespan.
  • Performance — heavy cryptography slows real-time operations and data processing, hindering the device’s primary functions.
  • Compliance — LWC helps IoT devices meet security regulations like GDPR and HIPAA with robust yet efficient security.

Key characteristics

  • Computational efficiency — faster, less compute per operation.
  • Memory efficiency — smaller RAM/ROM footprint.
  • Low power consumption — preserves battery; suits remote or unpowered deployments.
  • Security — high protection maintained despite reduced complexity.

How LWC is used in IoT

  • Data encryption — protects sensor data during transmission and storage.
  • Secure communication — TLS/SSL implemented with lightweight algorithms.
  • Digital signatures & key exchange — verifies authenticity of devices and data.
Good to know — the NIST lightweight standard

In 2023, NIST standardised the ASCON family as its Lightweight Cryptography standard — a one-line current-affairs point that impresses examiners when discussing LWC maturity.

Module 08 · Syllabus — authentication techniques

Authentication Techniques

Encryption protects data; authentication proves identity. Six mechanisms — from pre-shared keys to zero trust.

Definition

Authentication confirms the identity of a device or user. Strong IoT systems combine it with encryption — TLS, for example, does both.

  • Pre-shared Keys (PSK) — a shared secret for authentication; simple, but needs robust key security.
  • Usernames and passwords — fundamental; enhanced with multi-factor options.
  • Two-factor authentication (2FA) — a second form of verification beyond the password.
  • Digital certificates / PKI — public-key certificates provide digital identities and build trust.
  • Biometrics — unique biological traits (e.g., fingerprints) for user authentication.
  • Zero-Trust Network Access — verifies every device and user for every access request; no implicit trust.

8.1Pre-shared keys (PSK)

Definition

A Pre-Shared Key is a secret — such as a strong passphrase — shared between two devices (e.g., a device and a router) before they need to communicate. It serves as a shared secret for authentication and key derivation, proving the device’s identity and establishing encrypted communication. Security depends entirely on the strength of the PSK and how well it is managed.

Use cases in IoT: WPA/WPA2-PSK Wi-Fi networks (the passphrase is manually entered into the device to join) and device-to-device communication, where complex authentication mechanisms aren’t feasible.

✓ Advantages
  • Simplicity — a straightforward method, easy to implement and manage.
  • Cost-effective — no complex public-key infrastructure required; budget-friendly.
✕ Disadvantages
  • Key management — distributing and managing PSKs across thousands of devices is a serious challenge.
  • Security vulnerability — a compromised PSK can grant attackers access to the entire network of devices.
  • Limited scalability — less suitable for large or dynamic networks.

8.2Usernames & passwords

A critical but often weak link: many devices ship with universal default credentials that are easily exploited. You must change defaults to strong, unique passwords, use a password manager, and enable multi-factor authentication where possible.

Why default credentials are a problem:

  • Ubiquitous and predictable — the same defaults across many devices; easy for attackers to find and guess.
  • Common defaults — “admin”, “user”, “default” and “root” frequently appear in the top-10 passwords found in IoT attacks.
  • Lack of updates — many users never change them, leaving devices vulnerable.
  • Publicly available — manufacturers sometimes publish default passwords online or print them on the device itself.

How to secure your IoT devices (six steps):

  1. Change default passwords immediately during setup of any new IoT device.
  2. Use strong, unique passwords — complexity: mix uppercase, lowercase, numbers and special characters; uniqueness: a different password for each device and account.
  3. Use a password manager to generate and securely store strong, unique passwords.
  4. Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA) on IoT device accounts where available.
  5. Keep firmware updated to patch exploitable vulnerabilities.
  6. Secure your network — strong security settings on the home Wi-Fi router protect every connected device.

8.3Two-factor authentication (2FA)

Definition

2FA adds a critical layer of defence by requiring two different forms of identification to access an IoT device or service — significantly stronger than single-factor (password-only) authentication. It prevents unauthorized access even if a password is stolen, phished, or malware is present.

Common implementations in IoT:

  • Username/password + SMS/email code — the most common: the password (know) plus a one-time code sent to the registered phone or email (have).
  • Hardware tokens — dedicated tokens generating time-based PINs entered along with the password.
  • Mobile app authenticators — an alternative to SMS/email: the app generates one-time passwords directly on the smartphone.

Why 2FA is crucial for IoT: increased protection (an attacker must compromise both the password and the physical device); mitigates password weaknesses (weak, reused or compromised passwords); and IoT’s broader attack surface (connected homes, cities and businesses) makes robust authentication essential.

8.4Digital certificates & PKI

Definition

Digital certificates, issued by trusted Certificate Authorities (CAs), authenticate devices, encrypt data, and ensure the integrity of communications within an IoT network — preventing unauthorized access and securing data exchanged between numerous, often resource-constrained, IoT devices.

How PKI and digital certificates work in IoT:

  1. Identity and authentication — each IoT device is assigned a unique digital certificate containing its public key and identity.
  2. Certificate Authority (CA) — a trusted third-party organisation verifies the device’s identity and issues its digital certificate.
  3. Encryption and security — the certificate’s public key encrypts data sent to the device; the device’s paired private key decrypts it.
  4. Integrity and trust — certificates let devices digitally sign communications, verifying the source and proving messages haven’t been tampered with.
  5. Secure connections — only authenticated, trusted devices can connect to the network and communicate securely.

Enhanced security

Prevents unauthorized devices from joining the network; protects data from interception or modification.

Device & user authentication

Confirms the true identity of devices, users and services — they are who they claim to be.

Data integrity

Ensures data exchanged between devices has not been altered.

Scalability

PKI systems can manage the large number of devices in an IoT environment and secure their interactions.

8.5Biometrics

Definition

Biometrics authenticate users by unique physical or behavioural traits — like fingerprints or voice — more convenient for users and far harder for hackers to replicate than passwords. IoT systems often use multi-modal approaches for higher accuracy on resource-constrained devices.

How biometrics enhance IoT security:

  • Improved authentication — identity verified via unique personal traits; a strong defence against unauthorized access and identity theft.
  • Convenience and usability — no complex passwords to remember; a seamless, user-friendly experience.
  • Data protection — biometric templates and hashes can be securely stored and processed locally on the device, improving privacy.
  • Difficult to replicate — unlike passwords or keys, biometric markers are permanent and extremely hard to steal or replicate.
  • Increased accuracy — multimodal biometrics (e.g., face + voice) significantly increase recognition accuracy, especially in challenging conditions.

Common types: Physiological — fingerprint recognition (secure device/system access), facial recognition (smart homes identifying individuals), iris/palm-vein recognition (advanced methods mapping unique patterns for high-security authentication). Behavioural — voice recognition (smart assistants like Alexa and Google Home), typing habits and gait analysis (emerging methods tracking unique behavioural patterns).

Applications in IoT: smart homes (biometric scanners control entry and secure integrated systems); smart assistants (voice biometrics personalise experiences and restrict profiles on Alexa/Google Home); Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) (wearables monitor patient health, with secure authentication ensuring only authorised medical professionals access the data); and IoT-based attendance systems (secure, verifiable tracking in business organisations).

8.6Zero-Trust Network Access

Definition

A security model that verifies every device and user for every access request — preventing access based on implicit trust. Never trust, always verify.

Module 09 · Syllabus — privacy concerns & regulations

Privacy Concerns & Regulations

Security is protecting data from unauthorized access; privacy is the right to control how data is collected, used and shared. IoT stresses both.

1

Embedding data privacy

Privacy must be built into devices and data flows from the start (“privacy by design”) — retrofitting privacy across millions of already-deployed devices is extremely hard.

2

Proliferating devices

Every new device is a new collector of personal data and a new potential leak point. Keeping track of what is collected, where gets harder as fleets grow.

3

Increasing maintenance costs

Keeping data protected — encryption, secure storage, audits, updates, compliance — costs money, and the bill grows with every device. Under-investment creates risk.

4

Access control is difficult

IoT data is touched by many parties — manufacturers, service providers, third-party analytics. Enforcing fine-grained, industry-specific access control is complex in many industries.

5

A bad data culture

Organisations that over-collect, carelessly handle and never audit data create systemic privacy risk regardless of the technology deployed.

6

The ever-increasing scale of data

Volume, velocity and variety of IoT data outpace the ability to protect it. Even harmless-looking streams (smart-meter readings) aggregate into sensitive profiles — when you are home, what you do.

Regulations to name-drop

GDPR (EU) — comprehensive personal-data protection with heavy fines; applies to IoT products and services handling EU residents’ data. HIPAA (US) — protects health data; governs the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT). Link: lightweight cryptography helps constrained devices meet GDPR/HIPAA-grade protection (Module 07.4), and non-compliance leaves organisations vulnerable — with possible legal consequences after a breach (Module 04.5).

Exam tip

For “privacy concerns in IoT” answers: name all six headings, then expand any three with an example — scale (data aggregation), culture (over-collection), cost (maintenance). Close with GDPR/HIPAA for regulation marks.

IoT Security Best Practices

First the core hygiene checklist, then the famous “Top 10” — each with its advantages, exactly as examiners like it.

10.1Core hygiene — the foundation six

Secure the server infrastructure

Hardened backends protect the whole IoT estate.

Configure a firewall

For limited platform access — only necessary traffic passes.

Restrict device production access

Employees get least-privilege access to devices.

Handle regular system backups

Backups blunt ransomware’s leverage.

Use encryption

Everywhere data moves or rests.

Monitor system & business metrics

Anomalies surface early — in operations and in security.

10.2The Top 10 practices

1

Regularly update the IoT devices

IoT devices run software to perform their functions, and that software must be regularly updated to prevent attackers from exploiting known vulnerabilities.

Advantages: an effective patch-management process protects devices from vulnerabilities; RMM solutions schedule updates and patches and push them to all relevant devices automatically; the speed of addressing vulnerabilities improves — you patch far more than manual effort ever could.

2

Enable strong authentication

A robust password-protection strategy for IoT applications — including secure password generation and update processes. It is critical to change default passwords.

Advantages: multiple authentication factors keep devices safer; encrypted TLS communication reduces the chance of authentication data being compromised; malicious actors find it far harder to reach internal systems.

3

Secure IoT gateway solutions

Gateways are the critical checkpoint between devices and the broader internet: enforce stringent internet access policies so only authorised traffic reaches connected devices — keeping malware out of user connections and away from sensitive data.

Advantages: enhanced control & monitoring (spot threats early, act preventively); prevention of malware intrusion; a Secure Web Gateway (SWG) adds application control, deep inspection of HTTPS/SSL traffic, remote browser isolation and URL filtering; protection extends to cloud and remote environments; and data privacy improves — threat monitoring prevents leaks, while VPNs encrypt browsing data against hackers.

4

Monitoring

Monitor user activity, sign-in attempts, resource access and policy changes. Security event monitoring and logging lets organisations detect and respond to issues in a timely manner; set up alerts and dashboards for security metrics and easy tracking.

Advantages: keeps track of progress; detects underlying problems before they cause adverse effects; lets managers foresee breakdowns and conduct maintenance before it becomes urgent — minor tasks never become major issues.

5

Securing cloud APIs

Cloud APIs facilitate communication and data transfer between services — a compromised API can cause a significant data breach. Implement authentication, encryption, tokens and API gateways.

Advantages: strong authentication + encryption keeps data secure as it crosses the internet; gateways and tokens add a control layer that blocks unauthorized interactions; the integrity of data shared between servers and devices is maintained.

6

Track and manage the devices

You can’t manage devices without understanding how each one works and what it does — knowing your connected devices is the ultimate step to securing the IoT infrastructure.

Advantages: continuous monitoring software discovers and monitors devices; tracking raises alerts that protect devices from threats; productivity and safety both improve.

7

Carefully manage the device inventory

Device discovery and inventory improve security: knowing all connected devices means you can secure all of them — any unsecured device is a potential attack vector.

Advantages: maintains and protects devices from threats; automated discovery tools keep you ahead even with huge device populations; every device on the network gets best-practice security.

8

Update the credentials and passwords

Hackers can easily obtain and exploit passwords to gain control of devices — update credentials and passwords regularly.

Advantages: good password hygiene prevents account compromise; never reuse passwords across devices; keep any password storage encrypted and secure; add multifactor authentication.

9

Understand the endpoints

Every new IoT device — laptop, smartphone, cloud-based server — introduces a new endpoint. As device counts constantly grow, organisations struggle with effective endpoint security.

Advantages: identifying device profiles and IoT endpoints is the foundation of secure, healthy networks; endpoint security tools strengthen coverage; users get better safety.

10

Stay proactive with IoT security

Manage both active and inactive devices; always patch and maintain. Preventative measures are far more valuable to an organisation’s integrity than reactive ones.

Advantages: SNMP solutions monitor networks and raise alerts on potential problems; staying proactive keeps devices secure and the whole security practice manageable.

Jargon decoder — RMM · SWG · VPN · SNMP · ACL

RMM — Remote Monitoring & Management: schedules and automatically pushes updates/patches to all relevant devices.

SWG — Secure Web Gateway: enforces internet access policies; features application control, deep inspection of HTTPS/SSL traffic, remote browser isolation and URL filtering.

VPN — Virtual Private Network: encrypts browsing data, keeping internet activity private and protected from hackers.

SNMP — Simple Network Management Protocol: monitors networks and alerts on potential problems.

ACL — Access Control List: rules defining who or what may access a resource.

Memory hook — group the ten

Keep devices current (#1 updates, #8 credentials) · Know your fleet (#6 track, #7 inventory, #9 endpoints) · Guard the gates (#3 gateways, #5 APIs) · Watch everything (#4 monitoring) · Lock the front door (#2 authentication) · Stay ahead (#10 proactive).

11.1Rapid-fire definitions

EncryptionConverting data into an unreadable format to prevent unauthorized access.
CiphertextThe encrypted, unreadable form of data.
Symmetric encryptionOne shared secret key for encryption and decryption — AES, Blowfish.
Asymmetric encryptionPublic/private key pair; public encrypts, private decrypts — RSA, ECC.
Session keyA temporary symmetric key exchanged after a TLS handshake for fast bulk encryption.
Diffie–HellmanEstablishing a shared symmetric key without ever exchanging the key itself.
Digital signaturePrivate-key-created, public-key-verified proof of identity and integrity.
Digital certificateAn identity + public key, issued and signed by a Certificate Authority.
CA / PKITrusted third party issuing certificates / the system of CAs, certificates and keys enabling trust at scale.
TLSProtocol providing encrypted, authenticated, integrity-protected channels in transit; successor to SSL.
Lightweight cryptographyCryptography optimised for low power, memory and compute.
PSKA secret pre-shared between device and network for authentication and key derivation.
2FA / MFAAuthentication combining two (or more) factors: know / have / are.
BiometricsAuthentication via unique physiological or behavioural traits.
Shadow IoTUnauthorized, unvetted devices connected to a network without IT’s knowledge.
BotnetA network of malware-compromised, remotely controlled devices.
DDoSOverwhelming a target with traffic from many sources — an attack on availability.
RansomwareMalware that encrypts data and demands payment; double extortion adds a publish threat.
APTAdvanced Persistent Threat — a sophisticated attack that hides inside systems for long periods.
DNS spoofing / poisoningManipulating DNS records to redirect traffic from legitimate to malicious sites.
Zero trustVerify every device and user for every request; no access based on implicit trust.
Attack surfaceThe sum of all points where an attacker could attempt entry.

  1. Definition — encryption converts data into an unreadable format to prevent unauthorized access.
  2. Symmetric — one shared key; working (3 steps); examples AES/Blowfish; benefits speed + resource-friendliness; challenge: key distribution.
  3. Asymmetric — public/private pair; working (3 steps); RSA, ECC, Diffie–Hellman; applications: key exchange, digital signatures, entity authentication.

  6. Conclusion — real systems layer all three: e.g., MQTT over TLS with ECC certificates and LWC on constrained endpoints.
