01 CHAPTER 01 · FOUNDATIONS

Protocol Standardization for IoT

Before any protocol can be studied, understand the political landscape they were born into: a fragmented world of islands, consortia and competing forums — and why that is both a disease and a symptom of a healthy market.

1.1The IoT-A Project

IoT-A (Internet of Things Architecture) is one of the few efforts targeting a holistic architecture for all IoT sectors — a single reference model rather than another silo.

  • A consortium of 17 European organizations from 9 countries.
  • Its landmark contribution was surveying and summarizing the then-current status of IoT standardization, concluding the landscape was fundamentally broken (below).

1.2The Four Findings — Status of IoT Standardization

Finding 1Fragmented architecturesEach vertical built its own stack; nothing interoperates.
Finding 2No holistic approachNo complete, end-to-end IoT implementation proposal existed.
Finding 3Island solutionsRFID, sensor networks, SCADA etc. thrive as isolated islands.
Finding 4Little cross-sector reuseTechnology and knowledge rarely travel between sectors.

1.3Standardization is a Double-Edged Sword

This is a favourite theory question. The argument has two blades:

  • Critical to market development — standards create trust, interoperability, economies of scale and vendor-neutral ecosystems. Without them, no mass IoT market can exist.
  • But it may threaten innovation and inhibit change — once a standard is accepted by the market, enormous inertia locks it in, even when better technology appears.
Exam framing · quotable line

“Standardization and innovation are like yin & yang — they can be contradictory to each other in some cases, even though this observation is debatable.” Write this line; then argue both sides in one paragraph each.

1.4Structural Problems in the Standards World

  • Scoped silos by design: different consortia, forums and alliances standardize within their own limited scope. 3GPP covers only cellular wireless networks; EPCglobal’s middleware covers only RFID events.
  • Redundant competition: even within the same segment, more than one consortium works on standardization without enough communication with each other — some actively compete.
  • Gray zones in definition: some believe the IoT concept is well established, but it remains unclear which technologies should be included in the term at all.

1.5The Two (Probably Unanswerable) Open Questions

Q1 · Coordination problem

ICT standardization is a highly decentralized activity. How can the individual activities of the network of extremely heterogeneous standards-setting bodies be coordinated?

Q2 · Participation problem

It will become essential to allow all interested stakeholders to participate in the standardization process toward the IoT and to voice their respective requirements and concerns. How can this be achieved?

The source notes these two issues — for IoT standardization in particular and ICT standardization in general — may never have answers.

02 CHAPTER 02 · FOUNDATIONS

M2M & WSN Protocols

Machine-to-Machine is IoT’s industrial ancestor. Understanding its standardization efforts explains why the industry needed a cleaner, IP-centric generation of protocols.

2.1The Customization Problem

Most M2M applications are developed today in a highly customized fashion — each deployment is a bespoke engineering project with its own protocols, gateways and data formats. This is precisely the “island solution” problem from Chapter 1, seen from the engineering trench.

2.2The MSTF Reference Architecture

  • The high-level M2M architecture from the M2M Standardization Task Force (MSTF) includes fixed and other non-cellular wireless networks — not just cellular.
  • Because of this inclusiveness, it is effectively a generic, holistic IoT architecture, even though it is nominally an M2M architecture.
  • Terminology note: M2M and IoT are sometimes used interchangeably in the United States — a classic viva/short-question trap.

2.3The M2M Standards Activity Checklist

Memorize this list — it is asked as “what other M2M standards activities exist?” Answer in eight points:

A · Data transport protocol standards
M2MXMLJSONBiTXMLWMMPMDMP
  • OMA DM extension — extend Open Mobile Alliance Device Management to support M2M device-management protocol objects.
  • M2M device management and standardization of the M2M gateway.
  • M2M security and fraud detection.
  • Network APIs exposing M2M service capabilities.
  • Remote management of devices behind a gateway/firewall (a genuinely hard NAT-traversal problem — links to Chapter 6).
  • Open REST-based APIs for M2M applications (links to Chapter 12).
Memory hook

Transport → Manage (OMA DM, gateway) → Secure → Expose (APIs, REST, remote-behind-firewall). “T-M-S-E” — four verbs, eight bullets.

03 CHAPTER 03 · INDUSTRIAL & IDENTIFICATION

SCADA & RFID Protocols

SCADA represents the whole industrial automation arena — one of the acknowledged pillars of IoT. Its evolution from centralized control to distributed intelligent devices is a mini-history of IoT itself.

3.1What SCADA Is

Definition

SCADA — Supervisory Control And Data Acquisition. One of the IoT pillars, representing the entire industrial automation arena: centralized systems supervising remote telemetry, sensors and actuators over large geographic areas (power grids, pipelines, water networks).

  • The IEEE created a standard specification called IEEE Std C37.1™ (2007) for SCADA and automation systems.
  • In recent years, network-based industrial automation has greatly evolved with the use of Intelligent Electronic Devices (IEDs) — or “IoT devices” in our vocabulary — deployed in substations and power stations.

3.2From Centralized to Distributed

The key architectural shift: processing is now distributed. Functions that used to be performed at the control center can now be performed by the IED itself — i.e., M2M communication between devices, peer-to-peer, without round-trips to headquarters.

3.3The Restructured Electric Industry

Due to restructuring/deregulation, traditional vertically-integrated electric utilities were replaced by many specialized entities:

Fig 3.1 — The de-integrated electricity value chain. Generation, transmission and distribution became separate companies, coordinated by an ISO; IEDs inside substations now do locally what the control center once did centrally.

Mnemonic

G-T-D under ISO: “Generate, Transmit, Distribute — the ISO watches all.” Four entities, one chain.

3.4RFID Protocols (EPCglobal Family)

RFID standardization is the canonical example of a single-scope standards silo: EPCglobal’s middleware specification covers only RFID events — nothing else. Know this family at sketch level:

  • Air interface protocol — defines the physical dialogue between reader and tag (modulation, encoding, anti-collision for many tags in one reader field). The dominant standard: EPC Class-1 Gen-2 (EPCglobal), harmonized as ISO/IEC 18000-63, operating in UHF bands.
  • Tag memory structure — reserved (passwords), EPC (identity), TID (tag type), user memory banks.
  • Middleware / EPCIS — filters and captures RFID events and shares them with enterprise applications — the exact component whose narrow scope is criticized in Chapter 1.
04 CHAPTER 04 · INDUSTRIAL FIELD PROTOCOL

Modbus Protocol

The grandfather of industrial protocols — 1979, and still everywhere on factory floors. Exams love its four object types and its reasons for survival.

4.1Origins & Why Industry Adopted It

Definition

Modbus is a serial communications protocol originally published by Modicon (now Schneider Electric) in 1979, commonly available for connecting industrial electronic devices.

Four reasons for use in industrial environments — learn verbatim:

  1. Developed with industrial applications in mind.
  2. Openly published and royalty-free.
  3. Easy to deploy and maintain.
  4. Enables communication among many devices connected to the same network (multi-drop).

4.2The Four Object Types (Data Model)

Every piece of Modbus-addressable data falls into one of four categories, distinguished by access rights and size:

Object typeAccessSizeTypical use
CoilRead–write1 bitRelay / actuator ON-OFF state
Discrete inputRead-only1 bitDigital sensor: limit switch, door contact
Input registerRead-only16 bitsAnalog measurement: temperature, pressure
Holding registerRead–write16 bitsConfiguration value / setpoint
Mnemonic

“C-D-I-H” → Coils & Holding are writable; Discrete & Input are read-only; 1-bit objects are C/D, 16-bit objects are I/H. The axes of the table (access × size) uniquely determine each object.

4.3Protocol Versions

The Modbus family
Modbus RTUModbus ASCIIModbus TCPModbus over TCP (RTU/IP)Modbus over UDPModbus Plus (MB+)Pemex ModbusEnron Modbus
  • RTU — compact binary framing over serial (RS-232/485); the classic.
  • ASCII — human-readable hex characters over serial; more overhead, easier debugging.
  • TCP — same PDU encapsulated in TCP packets (port 502); today’s dominant variant.
  • Over UDP / Plus / Pemex / Enron — vendor- and region-specific extensions; naming alone suffices for exams.

05 CHAPTER 05 · SHORT-RANGE MESH

ZigBee Architecture

A high-level protocol suite riding on IEEE 802.15.4, built for small, low-power digital radios. Its layered architecture — and especially the roles of NWK, APS and ZDO — is a guaranteed long-answer question.

5.1What ZigBee Is

Definition

ZigBee is an IEEE 802.15.4-based specification for a suite of high-level communication protocols, used to create personal area networks with small, low-power digital radios.

Applications — home automation, medical device data collection, and other low-power, low-bandwidth uses.

5.2The Three-Section Architecture

ZigBee’s stack is divided into three sections:

  1. IEEE 802.15.4 — the PHYsical (PHY) and MAC layers.
  2. ZigBee layers — the Network layer (NWK), the ZigBee Device Object (ZDO), the Application Support sublayer (APS), and security management.
  3. Manufacturer application — manufacturers may adopt a standard ZigBee application profile or develop their own.
Manufacturer applicationstandard ZigBee application profile — or a custom one
ZDO · App objects (EP 1…240)+ security management
APS — application supportrequest · confirm · response services
NWK — network layerjoin/leave · routing · discovery
MAC — IEEE 802.15.4section ① of the architecture
PHY — IEEE 802.15.42.4 GHz radio

Fig 5.1 — ZigBee stack. The bottom two layers are owned by IEEE 802.15.4; everything above is defined by the ZigBee specification; the top belongs to the manufacturer.

5.3Network Layer (NWK)

Situated between the MAC layer and the APS, it provides four functions — learn all four:

  • Starting a network (coordinator duty).
  • Managing end devices joining or leaving a network.
  • Route discovery.
  • Neighbor discovery.

5.4APS — Application Support Sublayer

  • Provides services necessary for application objects (endpoints) and the ZDO.
  • Its data-transfer services to application objects come in three primitives: Request, Confirm, Response.

5.5Application Objects (Endpoints)

  • An application object defines input and output to the APS.
  • Worked example from the spec: a switch that controls a light — the switch is the input from the application object; the output is the light-bulb condition.
  • Each node can have 240 separate application objects (endpoints 1–240).

5.6ZDO — ZigBee Device Object

The ZDO handles control and management of application objects and performs the overall device-management tasks:

  • Determines the type of device in the network — end device, router, or coordinator.
  • Initializes the APS, the network layer, and the security service provider.
  • Performs device and service discovery.
  • Initializes the coordinator for establishing a network.
  • Security management.
  • Network management.
Mnemonic

T-I-D-C-S-N: Type, Init, Discovery, Coordinator, Security, Network — “This IoT Device Can Secure Networks.”

5.7End Nodes & Addressing Modes

  • Each end node (end device) can host multiple endpoints (EPs); each EP contains an application profile (e.g., home automation) and can control multiple devices or a single device.
  • ZigBee uses three addressing modes for transmission of information: direct, group, and broadcast addressing.
ModeMeaningExample
DirectMessage addressed to one specific endpoint on one specific nodeSwitch EP 3 → Lamp EP 1
GroupMessage addressed to a named group of endpoints across nodes“All lights in room 2”
BroadcastMessage delivered to every device in the networkNetwork-wide “permit join” command
06 CHAPTER 06 · THE IP FOUNDATION

IP Addressing: IPv4 → IPv6

IP delivers packets from one node to another without either knowing the other’s network type — it is ubiquitous, scalable and versatile, which is exactly why IoT wants to ride on it. But first, IPv4 had to be rescued from itself.

6.1The Exhaustion Problem

  • The Internet is the world’s largest public data network, doubling in size every nine months.
  • IPv4 defines a 32-bit address → 232 = 4,294,967,296 possible addresses.
  • The first problem is the eventual depletion of the IP address space.
  • The traditional classful model does not use the address space to its maximum potential — massive structural waste.

6.2Classful Addressing (1981)

When IP was standardized in September 1981, every attached system needed a unique 32-bit address built on a two-level hierarchy (network + host). Addresses were divided into 5 classes — Class A uses 8 bits for network-ID and 24 bits for host-ID, and so on for B and C:

ClassLeading bitsNet / Host bitsFirst-octet rangeDefault maskPurpose
A08 / 240–127/8 (255.0.0.0)Huge networks
B1016 / 16128–191/16 (255.255.0.0)Medium networks
C11024 / 8192–223/24 (255.255.255.0)Small networks
D1110—224–239—Multicast
E1111—240–255—Reserved / experimental

The fatal flaw: blocks are assigned along octet boundaries, so an organization needing 300 addresses had to take a whole Class B (65,534) — colossal wastage of IP addresses.

6.3Technique 1 — Subnetting

  • Introduces a three-level hierarchy: network, subnet, host.
  • The extended-network-prefix = classful network-prefix + subnet-number, traditionally identified by the subnet mask.
Worked example · subnetting

Given 192.168.10.0/24, create 4 subnets → borrow 2 host bits → /26, mask 255.255.255.192:

  • 192.168.10.0/26 — hosts .1 → .62
  • 192.168.10.64/26 — hosts .65 → .126
  • 192.168.10.128/26 — hosts .129 → .190
  • 192.168.10.192/26 — hosts .193 → .254

Each block: 64 addresses = 62 usable + network + broadcast.

6.4Technique 2 — CIDR

  • Classless Inter-Domain Routing eliminates traditional classful IP routing.
  • Supports the deployment of arbitrarily sized networks: routing information is advertised with a bit mask / prefix length specifying the number of leftmost contiguous bits in the network portion — e.g. 192.168.0.0/21.
Worked example · CIDR table lookup (the exam algorithm)

Entry 192.168.32.0/21 (mask 255.255.248.0). Destination 192.168.36.17:

  1. Extract the destination IP address.
  2. Boolean AND the address with the subnet mask for each entry: 36 = 00100100, 248 = 11111000 → AND = 00100000 = 32 → 192.168.32.0.
  3. Compare the result against the entry’s base address — it matches, so the packet is forwarded to the router for that base address.

6.5Technique 3 — NAT

  • Network Address Translation: each organization holds a single public IP address; inside, every host gets a private address unique within the organization, drawn from a reserved set (e.g., 10.0.0.0/8).
  • The NAT box rewrites private address:port pairs to its public address with distinct ports, keeping a translation table.

Fig 6.1 — NAT: many private addresses multiplexed onto one public address via port translation. This is why “remote management of devices behind a gateway/firewall” (Ch 2) is hard.

07 CHAPTER 07 · ADAPTATION LAYER

6LoWPAN

IPv6 over Low-power Wireless Personal Area Networks — the adapter plate that bolts the full Internet protocol suite onto tiny 802.15.4 radios. The mechanisms (compression, fragmentation, mesh addressing) are prime short-question material.

7.1What & Why

Definition

6LoWPAN is the adaptation layer developed to enable IPv6 over IEEE 802.15.4 networks. It is an open standard defined in RFC 6282 by the IETF. 6Lo (IPv6 over Bluetooth etc.) later made use of 6LoWPAN as its basis.

  • Provides a means of carrying packet data in the form of IPv6 over IEEE 802.15.4 and other networks.
  • Provides end-to-end IPv6 — hence direct connectivity to a huge variety of networks, including the Internet itself.
  • Character: very low cost, short range, low memory usage, low bit rate; comprises an edge router + sensor nodes. Even the smallest IoT devices (e.g., LED streetlights) can join the network and speak to the outside world.

7.2The MTU Mismatch — The Core Problem

To send IPv6 packets over 802.15.4, packet data must be converted to a format the 802.15.4 lower layer can handle. The crunch: IPv6 requires the MTU to be at least 1280 bytes, while an 802.15.4 frame can carry only ~127 bytes — hence the two key mechanisms: fragmentation and header compression.

7.3Mechanism 1 — Header Compression

  • 6LoWPAN uses header-compression mechanisms to reduce packet overhead when sending data over 802.15.4 links.
  • A typical 40-byte IPv6 header would consume a significant fraction of the frame payload, wasting precious energy and bandwidth.
  • Compression is defined to produce a lightweight encoding of IPv6 — eliding fields derivable from the 802.15.4 frame (e.g., link-layer addresses) or fixed by context.

7.4Mechanism 2 — Fragmentation

Large IPv6 datagrams are split into link-sized fragments. The fragment header is composed of three primary fields:

Datagram sizetotal (un-fragmented) payload size
Datagram tagidentifies which datagram this fragment belongs to
Datagram offsetthis fragment’s offset within the un-fragmented payload

Fig 7.1 — Fragment header fields. Size + tag let the receiver reassemble; offset says where each piece goes.

7.5Mechanism 3 — Mesh Addressing

For link-layer (mesh-under) forwarding, the mesh address header carries three fields:

  • Hop limit — limits the number of forwarding hops; decremented at each hop; once it reaches zero the packet is dropped (stops infinite loops).
  • Source address and destination address — indicate the IP endpoints (original origin and final target, not just the current radio hop).

7.6Routing — Mesh-Under vs Route-Over

Routing is the ability to send a data packet from one device to another, sometimes over multiple hops. Two categories are defined:

Mesh-underRoute-over
Where routing happensBelow IP, at the 6LoWPAN adaptation layerAt the IP layer itself
HowBroadcasting with the IPv6 protocol; fragments travel independently per hopEach node is an IP router; per-hop IP forwarding
See it asThe mesh looks like one big link to IPv6A genuine multi-hop IP network

7.7Networking Architecture & the Edge Router

Fig 7.2 — Uplink to the Internet is provided by the access point, an IPv6 router. The edge router’s workload is deliberately light.

Edge-router functions — the exam’s three functions:

  1. Exchange of data between 6LoWPAN devices and the Internet (forwarding IP datagrams between the different media — Ethernet, Wi-Fi, 3G, 4G — used in the IP network).
  2. Exchange of data among devices that are part of the 6LoWPAN network.
  3. Generate and maintain the 6LoWPAN network.

Device classes

  • Routers — route data to other nodes in the 6LoWPAN.
  • Hosts — end-point devices; sleepy devices check in with routers at regular intervals to save battery.

08 CHAPTER 08 · INFRASTRUCTURE PROTOCOL

RPL — IPv6 Routing Protocol

Routing Protocol for Low power and Lossy Networks: how a 6LoWPAN mesh actually finds paths. DODAG construction is the diagram to practice drawing by hand.

8.1What RPL Is

  • RPL stands for Routing Protocol for Low power and Lossy networks — designed for heterogeneous-traffic LLNs (WPANs, WSNs, Power Line Communication).
  • Based on the same standard as ZigBee and 6LoWPAN: IEEE 802.15.4. Supports both many-to-one and one-to-one communication.
  • It is a Distance-Vector routing protocol that builds a tree-like topology called a DODAG — Destination Oriented Directed Acyclic Graph — rooted towards one or more root (sink) nodes.
  • DAGs are created based on a user-specified Objective Function (OF), which defines how to find the best-optimized route among the sensor devices.
  • RPL was designed to support minimal routing needs and builds a robust topology over a lossy network; devices are connected so that no cycles exist in the connection graph.
  • In an RPL network each node acts as a router; routing is performed at the IP layer — every node examines each received IPv6 packet and picks the next hop from its IPv6 header.

8.2DODAG Construction — Step by Step

Fig 8.1 — DODAG topology. Each node knows its parent but holds no information about its children; RPL maintains at least one path from every node to the root via its preferred parent for faster paths.

  1. The root node starts sending its location information using DIO messages (DODAG Information Object) to all LLN levels — DODAG formation begins.
  2. Routers at each specific level register the parent path and their participation for each node.
  3. Each node propagates its own DIO messages, and the DODAG gets built gradually.
  4. Once constructed, the preferred parent obtained by the router is set as the default path towards the root.

8.3Modes of Operation

Storing modeNon-storing mode
Routing tablesAll nodes contain the entire routing table of the RPL domain — every node knows how to reach every other node directlyOnly the border router(s) hold the full table; all other nodes keep only their list of parents, used as default routes toward the border router
Downward trafficRouted by destination IPv6 address at each hopNodes always send packets up to the border router, which reaches the final destination using IP source routing
Trade-offMore memory/CPU per nodeAbbreviated tables save memory space and CPU; longer paths

One-line memory hook: “Storing = everyone smart · Non-storing = only the boss is smart.”

8.4Implementation — Contiki OS

  • RPL is implemented using the Contiki Operating System — open source, focused on low-power wireless IoT devices, first brought into the picture by Adam Dunkels.
  • Other similar operating systems: T-Kernel, EyeOS, LiteOS.
  • RPL occurs mostly in wireless sensors and networks.

8.5Main Features & Advantages

  • Scalability — accommodates big networks with low-power, lossy connectivity; suited to a range of IoT applications.
  • Multipoint-to-point traffic — sends data to a single destination from several devices within the LLN as required.
  • Quality of Service — methods for reliable packet delivery and congestion control.
  • Adaptive — modifies routes in response to link-quality and energy-availability changes.
  • Security — incorporates integrity protection, authentication, confidentiality and encryption within the LLN.
09 CHAPTER 09 · PATTERNS

IoT Communication Models

Four recurring conversation shapes between devices, brokers and servers. Every protocol in the next three chapters instantiates one of these — learn the shapes and the protocols become obvious.

01 · Request-Response — direct, synchronous exchange; client initiates and awaits the reply, mirroring HTTP. Suits immediate feedback: device configuration, remote-control commands, thermostats fetching weather, platforms querying device status.

02 · Push-Pull — combines proactive (push) and reactive (pull) mechanisms: data can be sent automatically or retrieved on demand. Suits systems needing both immediate notifications and scheduled updates by data type.

03 · Publish-Subscribe — highly scalable, decoupled framework; producers and consumers interact only through a message broker. Asynchronous one-to-many distribution; ideal where sensor data flows continuously to multiple consumers.

04 · Exclusive Pair — dedicated, persistent channel between two endpoints; continuous stateful interaction via TCP or Bluetooth, connection kept alive for the whole session. Suits mission-critical, latency-sensitive uses: industrial robotics, autonomous vehicles, medical monitoring.

10 CHAPTER 10 · APPLICATION LAYER

MQTT — Message Queue Telemetry Transport

The workhorse of IoT messaging: a featherweight publish/subscribe transport that runs on TCP and survives terrible networks. Master the broker diagram, the eight characteristics and the three QoS levels and you own this chapter.

10.1Overview

  • MQTT is an OASIS (Organization for the Advancement of Structured Information Standards) standard messaging protocol for the IoT; developed by IBM.
  • Designed as an extremely lightweight publish/subscribe messaging transport, ideal for connecting remote devices with a small code footprint and minimal network bandwidth.
  • Used across industries: automotive, manufacturing, telecommunications, oil & gas, etc. — sending data from sensors to middleware and applications.
  • Works on top of TCP/IP, providing reliable delivery of data. Three main components: Subscriber · Publisher · Broker.
  • Simple, compact, binary packet payload.

10.2Architecture — Broker, Topics, Pub/Sub

Fig 10.1 — The heart of MQTT. The broker is the central server: it receives published messages, filters them by topic, and forwards them to clients subscribed to those topics. A broker can handle thousands of concurrently connected clients depending on implementation.

  • Topics look like file paths and can be chosen almost freely — each message is published to a topic; subscribers receive everything published on topics they subscribed to.
  • Publishers and subscribers are both MQTT clients — the same client can do both; clients talk only to brokers, never to each other.
  • Any device or programme running an MQTT library can be a client — from an Arduino microcontroller to full cloud application servers.
  • Publish/sub decouples producers from consumers — the third party (broker) manages all relationships — unlike the conventional client-server model that communicates directly with an endpoint.

10.3How It Behaves Under Failures

  • When the broker and a subscribing client lose contact, the broker stores messages in a buffer and delivers them once the subscriber is back up (persistent sessions — the broker keeps all persistent clients’ sessions, including missed messages and subscriptions).
  • MQTT caterers for (wireless) network disruptions — a core design goal.
  • If the publishing client abruptly disconnects, the broker may cut off communication with subscribers and send them a cached message containing the publisher’s instructions — the Last Will (below).

10.4The Eight Characteristics

  1. Lightweight message queueing and transport protocol.
  2. Asynchronous communication model with messages (events).
  3. Low overhead — 2-byte header — for low-network-bandwidth applications.
  4. Publish/Subscribe (PubSub) model.
  5. Decoupling of data producer (publisher) and consumer (subscriber) through topics (message queues).
  6. Simple protocol, aimed at low complexity, low power, low-footprint implementations.
  7. Runs on connection-oriented transport (TCP).
  8. Caters for (wireless) network disruptions.

10.7Advantages vs Disadvantages

Advantages
  • Lightweight protocol — quick to create, efficient data transport
  • Minimal data packets → low network usage; effective data dispersion
  • Effective remote sensing & control; prompt message delivery
  • Minimizes power consumption; maximizes network capacity
  • Fixed header of 2 bytes, payload up to 256 MB
Disadvantages
  • Slower send cycles than CoAP
  • Resource discovery via flexible topic subscription (CoAP has a more reliable discovery system)
  • No built-in encryption — relies on TLS/SSL
  • Building an internationally scalable MQTT network is challenging
Deep dive · MQTT-SN (MQTT for Sensor Networks)

For devices where even TCP is too heavy, MQTT-SN adapts MQTT to run directly over UDP or ZigBee/802.15.4: topics become short pre-registered IDs instead of strings, and there is a gateway that transparently bridges MQTT-SN clients to a normal MQTT broker. Expect it as a one-mark MCQ under “application protocols”.

11 CHAPTER 11 · APPLICATION LAYER

CoAP — Constrained Application Protocol

“The web, shrunk to fit a microcontroller.” A UDP-based, RESTful transfer protocol for constrained nodes and networks — with one method HTTP never had: Observe.

11.1Overview

Definition

CoAP is a specialized web transfer protocol for constrained nodes and constrained networks, for M2M applications. Several header functionalities are redesigned for M2M: built-in resource discovery, IP multi-casting support, a native push model, and asynchronous message exchange. Uses UDP transport, a compact binary header, and a simple request/response model.

11.3Key Features

  • UDP-based — less overhead than TCP, efficient for low-power devices on limited bandwidth.
  • HTTP-like (RESTful) semantics — familiar methods; resource-oriented: every object is a resource with a unique URI.
  • Compact binary header; client-server request/response model; asynchronous messaging (no per-message TCP handshake).
  • Reliability mechanisms: confirmable (CON) messages and acknowledgments (ACKs) — reliable delivery even on lossy networks.
  • Resource observation — clients “observe” resources and get automatic updates on state change.
  • Block-wise transfers — split large payloads into manageable blocks.
  • Built-in resource discovery; multicast communication; proxy & caching support (CoAP ↔ HTTP mapping lets constrained devices interwork with the broader web).
  • Energy efficiency — minimizes energy consumption; mechanisms to determine if a device is powered on or off.

CoAP messages are encoded in binary. Header + payload + optional section; the header is exactly 4 bytes (32 bits), fixed for every message:

12 CHAPTER 12 · APPLICATION LAYER

REST, AMQP & Friends

Two more members of the application-layer family: REST — the architectural style behind every IoT HTTP API — and AMQP — the heavyweight, transactional messaging backbone of the industrial backend.

12.1REST in IoT

Definition — note it is a style, not a protocol

REST (Representational State Transfer) is an architectural style for designing network-based software applications. It uses the stateless, request-response model of the web to create RESTful APIs, letting devices and web services communicate via standard HTTP methods, with data typically in JSON or XML.

How REST works in IoT — five steps

  1. Client-server architecture — a client (mobile app / web server) sends a request to a server (IoT platform or device).
  2. HTTP methods specify the action: GET retrieve · POST create · PUT update · DELETE remove.
  3. Resources & URIs — each device or sensor is a resource with a unique URI.
  4. Data formats — standardized, most commonly JSON or XML.
  5. Request-response — the server processes and answers with status + requested data.

Why REST is used in IoT — four reasons

  • Simplicity — leverages the familiar HTTP protocol.
  • Lightweight — less resource-intensive; suits devices with limited processing power.
  • Statelessness — each request carries all information needed; no complex session state — ideal for devices that frequently connect/disconnect.
  • Interoperability — standardized way for different devices, applications and cloud services to interact.

12.2AMQP — Advanced Message Queuing Protocol

Definition

AMQP is an open replacement of existing proprietary messaging middleware, providing reliability and interoperability. Developed by JPMorgan Chase. It uses a topic-based publish/subscribe model — exchanges route messages directly by topic and also by headers. Relies on TCP for reliable communication and secures it with TLS and SASL.

Fig 12.1 — AMQP architecture. Bindings define the connection between exchange and queue, specifying how messages are routed.

How AMQP works in IoT — five mechanisms

  1. Broker-based architecture — a message broker manages message flow.
  2. Publish-subscribe — devices publish to an exchange, which routes to queues based on routing rules.
  3. Message queues — store messages until a consuming client application retrieves and processes them.
  4. Bindings — define the exchange↔queue connection and routing.
  5. Reliable delivery — several guarantee levels: at-most-once, at-least-once, exactly-once.

Advantages — reliability & security (acknowledgments, TCP/TLS end-to-end); advanced routing via exchange types (direct, fanout, topic, headers); interoperability as an open standard; transactionality for critical data. Considerations — significant overhead (unsuitable for low-power edge devices — MQTT wins there); complexity suits backend/cloud/data centres; excels in industrial IoT, security-critical and fault-tolerant systems.

13 CHAPTER 13 · LONG RANGE

LoRa & LoRaWAN

Kilometres of range on a coin-cell battery, in license-free spectrum. The final protocol family of the unit — and the star of the case study.

13.1What LoRa Is

Definition

LoRa (Long Range) is a low data rate, low power wireless platform technology for building IoT networks — a patented digital wireless data communication technology developed by Cycleo of Grenoble, France. It uses chirp spread spectrum (CSS) modulation, which trades data rate for sensitivity — that is the secret of its range.

  • Transmits over license-free sub-GHz (Megahertz) radio bands: 169 MHz, 433 MHz, 868 MHz (Europe), 915 MHz (North America).
  • Enables very-long-range transmissions — more than 10 miles in rural areas — with low power consumption.
  • The LoRa Alliance developed the LoRaWAN protocol for mobile network operators who want to use unlicensed spectrum to communicate with IoT devices in their network.

13.2Star Topology — Star-of-Stars

Fig 13.1 — LoRa’s star topology: end nodes talk to gateways (LoRa radio); gateways forward to a central network server over standard IP backhaul. Uplink redundancy across gateways improves reliability.

13.3LoRaWAN

Definition

LoRaWAN is a Low Power Wide Area Network (LPWAN) specification intended for wireless, battery-operated things in a regional, national or global network. It targets key IoT requirements: secure bidirectional communication, mobility and localization services.

  • Provides long-range communication (up to 15 km) between sensors and base stations.
  • Fully bidirectional — examples: street lighting, smart irrigation, energy optimization, home automation.
  • Deployed on cost-free ISM bands (industrial, scientific, medical) — any service provider can deploy and operate LoRaWAN networks without acquiring a frequency license.

13.4Advantages vs Disadvantages

LoRaWAN advantages
  • Low-powered sensors cover large areas using low-power devices
  • Uses unlicensed ISM frequencies
  • A single gateway handles thousands of end devices/nodes — suitable for M2M/IoT applications
  • Governed by an alliance (vendor-neutral ecosystem)
  • Long range enables smart-city-scale solutions; easy to set up, fast deployment
  • Two layers of AES security — one for the network, one for the application
LoRaWAN disadvantages
  • Not for large data payloads — no audio or video support
  • Limited line-of-sight communications
  • Not ideal for real-time applications requiring low latency / bounded jitter
14 CHAPTER 14 · CASE STUDY

LoRa-Based Smart Irrigation System

The unit’s capstone: apply everything — sensors, LoRa, gateways, servers, actuation — to one concrete farm. Structure your long answer exactly like this chapter.

14.1Problem & Objective

  • Problem: conventional irrigation is timer-based and blind to actual soil condition → over-watering (wasted water & energy, leached nutrients) or under-watering (crop stress). Farms are large, remote, and have no power or wired connectivity in the field.
  • Objective: a closed-loop system that measures soil moisture (and weather) per zone and opens/closes water valves automatically, communicating over kilometers on battery power.
  • Why LoRa (the thesis of the case study): multi-kilometre range, years of battery life, license-free ISM band, one gateway serves thousands of nodes, bidirectional (downlink valve commands!) — every LoRaWAN advantage maps directly onto a farm’s constraints.

14.2System Architecture

Fig 14.1 — Four tiers: field nodes (sensors + valve controller with LoRa radio) → LoRa gateway (bridging radio ↔ IP) → network + application servers (decision logic) → user dashboard. Reference video walkthrough: youtu.be/sygF8RQSfkQ

14.3Data Flow — The Exam Workflow

  1. Sense: soil-moisture, temperature and humidity nodes wake on schedule, sample, and transmit small packets (a few bytes) via LoRa to the gateway — then return to deep sleep to conserve battery.
  2. Relay: the gateway (mains-powered, on a mast for line-of-sight) forwards packets over IP backhaul (Ethernet/cellular) to the network server, which de-duplicates messages received via multiple gateways.
  3. Decide: the application server compares moisture readings against crop- and zone-specific thresholds (optionally fusing weather data to skip irrigation before rain).
  4. Actuate: the server issues a bidirectional downlink command through the gateway to the valve node, opening/closing the solenoid for a computed duration.
  5. Verify & visualize: flow-meter feedback confirms water delivered; the dashboard logs history, raises alerts, and lets the farmer override manually.

14.4Why LoRa Fits — Mapping to Protocol Properties

14.5Outcomes & Limitations

  • Outcomes: major water savings (typically 30–50% in reported deployments), energy savings, improved crop yield uniformity, labor-free operation, scalable zone-by-zone expansion.
  • Limitations (inherit from Ch 13): not suitable for high-rate data or video monitoring; latency and duty-cycle limits make sub-second valve control unrealistic; terrain can block line of sight.

Exactly-once delivery: PUBLISH → PUBREC → PUBREL → PUBCOMP — a 4-way handshake guaranteeing no loss and no duplicates.

Answer

MQTT message the client registers with the broker; published by the broker on the client’s unexpected disconnect — enables graceful failure detection.

The CoAP method absent from HTTP: GET + observe option; the server pushes every update of the resource to the client — responses direct or piggy-backed.

Answer

6LoWPAN routing categories: mesh-under routes below IP at the adaptation layer (broadcasting with IPv6); route-over routes at the IP level with every node an IP router.
