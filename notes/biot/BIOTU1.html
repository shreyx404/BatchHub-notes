Part A · Chapter 01

Definition of the Internet of Things

Before anything else, an examiner wants the canonical definition — reproduced accurately, then unpacked phrase by phrase.

Definition · memorise verbatim
“A dynamic global network infrastructure with self-configuring capabilities based on standard and interoperable communication protocols where physical and virtual ‘things’ have identities, physical attributes and virtual personalities, use intelligent interfaces, are seamlessly integrated into the information network, and often communicate data associated with users and their environments.” — ITU (International Telecommunication Union)

1.1Unpacking the definition

PhraseWhat it actually tells you
Dynamic global network infrastructureA worldwide, constantly changing fabric of connected nodes — not a fixed, static network.
Self-configuring capabilitiesDevices join, leave, discover services and set themselves up without manual administration.
Standard, interoperable protocolsMulti-vendor devices communicate through commonly agreed protocol stacks.
Things with identities, attributes, personalitiesEvery object — physical or virtual — is individually addressable and describable.
Intelligent interfacesSmart front-ends (sensors, APIs, UIs) through which things connect and communicate.
Seamlessly integrated into the information networkObjects become first-class citizens of the Internet — invisibly woven in.
Communicate data about users and environmentsData is context-aware: user location, surroundings, status, events.

1.2Four alternative definitions (frequently asked)

Wikipedia

“The Internet of Things, also called The Internet of Objects, refers to a wireless network between objects; usually the network will be wireless and self-configuring, such as household appliances.”

WSIS 2005

“By embedding short-range mobile transceivers into a wide array of additional gadgets and everyday items, enabling new forms of communication between people and things, and between things themselves.”

IoT 2008 (Zurich)

“The term ‘Internet of Things’ has come to describe a number of technologies and research disciplines that enable the Internet to reach out into the real world of physical objects.”

IoT in 2020

“Things having identities and virtual personalities operating in smart spaces using intelligent interfaces to connect and communicate within social, environmental and user contexts.”

Part A · Chapter 02

Characteristics of IoT

Six defining characteristics — a guaranteed exam question in some form. Learn the list, then one sentence of elaboration for each.

01 · Dynamic and self-adapting

Devices adapt their behaviour to changing contexts and operating conditions — day/night, indoor/outdoor, motion, or the presence of other devices.

02 · Self-configuring

Thousands of devices can join and leave the network: automatic discovery, autonomous setup, and even automatic software/firmware updates — without human administrators.

03 · Interoperable protocols

Devices from different vendors speak through standard, interoperable communication protocols, enabling modularity of hardware and software.

04 · Unique identity

Every thing has a unique identifier (e.g., an IP address or an EPC) — it is individually addressable, identifiable, and can be queried or controlled remotely.

05 · Integrated into the information network

Things register themselves, describe their capabilities, and share data seamlessly with the network — enabling autonomic, system-level behaviour.

06 · Communicates contextual data

Devices frequently communicate data associated with users and their environments — the source of both their value and their privacy risk.

Mnemonic

“D-S-I-U-I-C” — Dynamic, Self-configuring, Interoperable, Unique identity, Integrated network, Contextual data. Say it as a chain: “Dynamic Systems Interoperate; Unique Identities Communicate.”

Exam tip

If asked for characteristics “with examples”: pair each with one — self-configuring → a new smart bulb joins the home Wi-Fi automatically; unique identity → every package has an EPC tag; interoperability → a ZigBee sensor’s data still reaches a cloud app over HTTP.

Part A · Chapter 03

Physical design of IoT

The physical design answers “what is actually built?” — it is defined by the Things (IoT devices) and the protocols that connect them.

3.1What is a “Thing”?

The “Things” in IoT usually refer to IoT devices which have unique identities, perform remote sensing and actuating, and have monitoring capabilities. An IoT device may consist of several interfaces for connections to other devices, both wired and wireless:

I/O interfaces

For sensors (and actuators) — ADC, GPIO, I²C, SPI, PWM.

Internet connectivity interfaces

WiFi, Ethernet, BLE, cellular modems.

Memory and storage interfaces

RAM, flash, SD cards for buffering and local data.

Audio/video interfaces

Microphones, cameras, codecs for rich media capture.

3.2Four ways an IoT device handles data

  1. Exchange data with other connected devices and applications — directly or indirectly.
  2. Collect and process locally — data from other devices is processed on the device itself.
  3. Send to servers / cloud back-ends — centralized servers or cloud applications process the data.
  4. Hybrid — perform some tasks locally and others within the IoT infrastructure, based on temporal and space constraints (the essence of fog/edge computing).
Recall

Physical design = Things + Protocols. A Thing = unique identity + remote sensing/actuating + monitoring. Data handling: exchange · local · cloud · hybrid.

5.3Network layer — addressing and routing

IPv4

32-bit addresses → ~4.3 billion total. Exhausted long before 50-billion-device forecasts — the original motivation for IoT moving to IPv6.

IPv6

128-bit addresses → ~3.4 × 1038. Every grain of sand on Earth could have billions of addresses — enough for every “thing”, forever.

6LoWPAN

“IPv6 over Low-power Wireless Personal Area Networks.” An adaptation layer that squeezes IPv6 onto 802.15.4 radios via header compression and fragmentation; supports mesh forwarding.

5.5Application layer — the seven protocols

HTTP — Hypertext Transfer Protocol

TCP · request–response · REST. The web’s protocol; verbose headers make it heavy for constrained nodes, but universal for cloud APIs (HTTPS over TLS).

CoAP — Constrained Application Protocol

UDP · REST for tiny things. 4-byte binary header; GET/POST/PUT/DELETE; asynchronous exchanges; “Observe” option gives server-push; discovery via /.well-known/core. Designed for constrained devices and networks (RFC 7252).

MQTT — Message Queuing Telemetry Transport

TCP · publish–subscribe via a central broker. 2-byte fixed header; hierarchical topics (home/room1/temp); QoS 0/1/2; retained messages and Last-Will; the de-facto standard for telemetry up to the cloud.

WebSocket

TCP · full-duplex over a single connection. Begins as an HTTP “Upgrade” handshake, then stays open — ideal for low-latency push to dashboards and live monitors.

XMPP — Extensible Messaging and Presence Protocol

TCP · XML stanzas (formerly Jabber). Built for presence and instant messaging; addresses via JIDs (user@domain/resource); federated servers; strong for person-to-thing communication.

DDS — Data Distribution Service

Brokerless peer-to-peer publish–subscribe (over RTPS). Data-centric with rich QoS policies (deadline, liveliness, durability); built for real-time machine-to-machine flows — industrial IoT, robotics, autonomous vehicles.

AMQP — Advanced Message Queuing Protocol

TCP · store-and-forward queuing. Exchanges route messages to queues via bindings; transactions and guaranteed delivery; the enterprise backbone (finance, order pipelines) that IoT systems plug into.

Master comparison

MQTT/AMQP = brokered pub-sub · DDS = brokerless pub-sub · HTTP/CoAP = request–response · WebSocket = full-duplex push · XMPP = XML presence. CoAP and DDS ride on UDP; the rest on TCP.

Deep dive · MQTT QoS levels and why they matter
  • QoS 0 — at most once: fire and forget; no acknowledgement; duplicates impossible, loss possible.
  • QoS 1 — at least once: sender retries until PUBACK; duplicates possible.
  • QoS 2 — exactly once: a four-way handshake (PUBREC → PUBREL → PUBCOMP); costliest, safest.
  • Retained messages: the broker keeps the last message per topic so new subscribers get an instant snapshot.
  • Last Will and Testament: a message the broker publishes on the client’s behalf if it disconnects unexpectedly — how IoT systems detect dead devices.
  • Ports: 1883 (plain), 8883 (TLS). MQTT-SN adapts MQTT for sensor networks over UDP/ZigBee.
Deep dive · how CoAP stays tiny

CoAP compresses the REST model into a 4-byte binary header and pairs it with UDP. Reliability is recovered selectively through confirmable (CON) messages — retransmitted with exponential backoff — while non-confirmable (NON) messages are pure fire-and-forget. The Observe extension lets a client subscribe to a resource, giving push semantics without a broker; a built-in HTTP/CoAP proxy mapping lets constrained devices join the wider web.

Logical design of IoT

The logical design is the abstract representation of entities and processes — deliberately silent about low-level implementation details.

Definition
“Logical design of an IoT system refers to an abstract representation of the entities and processes without going into the low-level specifics of the implementation.” — Unit 1 syllabus formulation

An IoT system comprises a number of functional blocks that provide the system the capabilities for identification, sensing, actuation, communication and management. Textbooks decompose these into six blocks:

6.1Communication APIs — the two patterns

Request–Response (sync)
  • Client asks, server answers — stateless conversations.
  • Used by HTTP and CoAP.
  • Simple to reason about; wasteful for frequent pushes.
Publish–Subscribe (async)
  • Producers publish to topics; subscribers receive what they care about — decoupled in time, space and identity.
  • Used by MQTT, AMQP (brokered) and DDS (brokerless).
  • Scales beautifully for telemetry fan-in/out.

6.2Technology roadmap — where IoT came from

2000smonitoring era

WSN · M2M telemetry

2010sconnectivity era

IoT · cloud · smartphones

Recall

Logical design = abstraction, no implementation detail. Six blocks: Device · Communication · Services · Management · Security · Application. Two API patterns: request–response and publish–subscribe.

Deep dive · inductive coupling vs backscatter

Inductive LF / HF tags live in the reader’s near field. The reader coil generates an alternating magnetic field; the tag coil, placed nearby, induces a current (Faraday’s law) — effectively a loose transformer. The tag talks back by varying the load on its coil (load modulation), which the reader senses.

UHF tags operate in the far field of electromagnetic waves. The tag antenna captures the wave, rectifies it for power, and re-radiates a portion with its antenna impedance modulated — backscatter — encoding its ID onto the reflection. This is why UHF reaches metres while inductive systems reach centimetres.

Each tag carries a stored code — commonly the EPC (Electronic Product Code), typically 96 bits encoding header, manager, object class and serial — which directory services (like the Object Naming Service) can resolve to richer data.

History and timeline of IoT

Dates and names are pure exam currency. Learn the five headline milestones plus the two “pre-history” anecdotes.

1982
The Carnegie Mellon Coke machine

The first ARPANET-connected appliance — students remotely checked whether drinks were cold. IoT before the name existed.

1990
John Romkey’s Internet toaster

A toaster controllable over the Internet — the legendary demo of “connecting a thing”.

1997
ITU Internet Reports series launched

The series begins under the title “Challenges to the Network”. Its seventh report — published in 2005 — becomes “The Internet of Things”.

1999
Kevin Ashton coins “Internet of Things” · Auto-ID Center founded at MIT

Ashton used the phrase in a Procter and Gamble supply-chain presentation; the Auto-ID Center drove RFID-EPC research.

2003
EPCglobal founded

The commercial successor to the Auto-ID Center (under GS1), standardizing the Electronic Product Code.

2005
WSIS conference (Tunis) · ITU IoT report

Four important technologies of IoT proposed: RFID, sensor technology, smart technology, nanotechnology.

2008
First International Conference on IoT — IoT 2008, Zurich

Also 2008–09: connected devices surpassed the human population (Cisco IBSG estimate).

Exam tip

Common trap: the ITU series launched in 1997, but the report actually titled “The Internet of Things” is the 2005 seventh report, unveiled at WSIS. Read date questions twice.

M2M and IoT — applications by industry

Machine-to-Machine communication is IoT’s direct ancestor — and still its industrial workhorse.

12.1M2M vs IoT

M2M
  • Direct device-to-device (often point-to-point) links
  • Proprietary or cellular connectivity; device-centric
  • Limited scale; data often trapped in silos
  • Minimal interoperability, minimal analytics
IoT
  • IP-based, cloud-centric architecture
  • Interoperable standard protocols (MQTT, CoAP…)
  • Massive scale; data aggregated across systems
  • Analytics and services built on top of the data

Exam line: M2M is widely regarded as a subset and precursor of IoT — “IoT = M2M + IP + cloud + data analytics.”

Application scenarios

Four canonical scenarios — home, shopping, health, transport — plus two real-world “IoT in the wild” examples.

14.1Intelligent home

Lighting, HVAC, security, entertainment and appliances are networked and automated — energy managed automatically, the home controllable remotely, routines that adapt to presence. The level-1-to-level-6 templates of Chapter 07 all find expression here.

14.2Scenario: shopping — “the goods introduce themselves”

Part B · Chapter 15

State of the art — the four enabling technologies

WSIS 2005’s four pillars, and the reason they form a chain: identify → sense → process → shrink.

RFID

To identify and track the data of things. Contactless, unique, item-level identity — the nervous system of supply chains.

Sensor technology

To collect and process the data to detect changes in the physical status of things — the bridge between physical and virtual worlds.

Smart technology

To enhance the power of the network by devolving processing capabilities to different parts of the network — intelligence pushed outward, toward the edge.

Nano technology

To make smaller and smaller things have the ability to connect and interact — connectivity at scales where “things” become nearly invisible.

Mnemonic

“Identify → Sense → Process → Shrink” — RFID identifies, Sensors sense, Smart tech processes, Nanotech shrinks. The four pillars in the exact order of the data lifecycle.

15.1Sensor technology — “the magic of IoT”

  • The ability to detect changes in the physical status of things is essential for recording changes in the environment.
  • Wireless sensor technology bridges the gap between the physical and virtual worlds, enabling things to respond to changes in their physical environment.
  • Sensors collect data, generate information and raise awareness about context.
  • The sensor market spans: MEMS-based sensors, optical sensors, ambient light sensors, gesture sensors, proximity sensors, touch sensors, fingerprint sensors, and more.

Tools and case studies

From the free aggregation platform every maker starts on, to four worked engineering stories — including two with named machine-learning methods.

18.1ThingSpeak

Free online data aggregation platform
  • Collects data from sensors — your “Things” — with instant visualization.
  • Popular with experimenters: more than 50,000 users; used by makers, education and prototyping engineers.
  • Analyze: MATLAB integration runs scheduled MATLAB code on incoming data.
  • Act: e.g., tweet a message when the backyard temperature reaches 32 degrees.

18.2Four worked examples

Weather monitoring

Objective: measure, explore and discover weather patterns; provide a niche weather service. Solution: Arduino station with weather sensors + cloud-based aggregation and analysis.

Traffic monitoring

Objective: discover traffic patterns; provide live local traffic information. Solution: Raspberry Pi + webcam, with automated deployment of vision algorithms on the embedded sensor — analytics pushed to the edge node itself.

BuildingIQ — predictive energy optimization

Opportunity: real-time, cloud-based system helping commercial building owners cut HVAC energy. Data: 3–12 months from power meters, thermometers, pressure sensors + weather and energy cost — billions of data points. ML: SVM regression, Gaussian mixture models, k-means clustering. Optimization: multi-objective, constrained. Benefit: typical energy consumption reduced ~15%.

iSonea — cloud and embedded analytics

Opportunity: acoustic respiratory monitoring for wheeze detection and asthma management. Flow: captures 30 s of windpipe sound → processes locally to clean up and reduce ambient noise → spectral processing and pattern detection on the iSonea cloud server → feedback on the patient’s smartphone. Benefit: eliminates error-prone self-reporting and doctor visits.

Exam tip

BuildingIQ and iSonea together illustrate the edge-vs-cloud analytics spectrum: iSonea splits work between device (denoising) and cloud (pattern detection); BuildingIQ is cloud-centric machine learning. Quote the named algorithms — SVM regression, Gaussian mixtures, k-means — examiners reward specificity.

Deep dive · model-based design and the toolchain

Real IoT systems blend different modelling approaches: physical modelling (schematics), block-diagram simulation (Simulink), embedded digital electronics (C++, Verilog), FPGA/IC integration, data-driven algorithm development (MATLAB), followed by implementation and V&V — verification and validation.

Simulink models run directly on low-cost target hardware: Raspberry Pi, Gumstix Overo, PandaBoard, Arduino, LEGO MINDSTORMS NXT, BeagleBoard — a pick-and-place robot demo being the classic classroom example.

On languages: an IEEE Spectrum ranking used 12 metrics (sources including Google, CareerBuilder and GitHub) to compare popularity — the lesson being “pick the right language for the task”, not the trendiest one.

Underneath it all, machine learning is everywhere: image recognition, speech recognition, stock prediction, medical diagnosis, data analytics, robotics — the “A” and “B” of IoT’s ABCD.

Wireless sensor networks and the evolving network

The WSN is IoT’s oldest ancestor — and the network beneath it is being extended, expanded and enhanced simultaneously.

19.1WSN architecture

SENSOR NODESn1 … nN · low-power

one or more sensors each; may differ in type; actuators possible

SINK NODEmulti-hop collector

nodes relay data hop-by-hop toward the sink

GATEWAYprotocol bridge

lifts WSN traffic onto the Internet

CORE NETWORKe.g., the Internet
END-USERcomputer services

storage, analysis, visualization, action

Key points: WSNs typically run on low-power devices; each node carries one or more sensors of possibly different types (or actuators); data funnels through the sink and gateway into the core network for end-users and computer services.

19.2How the networks are changing

Extensions

More nodes, more connections — IPv6 and 6LoWPAN letting the address space scale to billions.

Expansions

Broadband everywhere — fat pipes for fat sensor data.

Enhancements

Smart networks · data-centric and content-oriented networking · context-aware (autonomous) systems.

The one-line summary
“Any TIME, any place + any THING — M2M, IoT: billions of interconnected devices, everybody connected.” — the network-evolution slogan to memorise

19.3People ↔ things, things ↔ things

People connecting to things
  • Motion sensors and ECG sensors stream to the Internet
  • A person queries or receives alerts from remote devices
Things connecting to things
  • Machines coordinate directly with machines
  • Complex, heterogeneous resources and networks interoperate

Meanwhile, sensor devices became widely available as programmable devices and off-the-shelf gadgets, and ever more things connect: home and daily-life devices, business and public infrastructure, health-care equipment.

Part B · Chapter 23

Criticism — privacy, autonomy and control

The counter-argument every good essay needs: serious thinkers doubt the promise of ubiquitous computing.

Peter-Paul Verbeek — philosophy of technology, Netherlands

Technology already influences our moral decision making, which in turn affects human agency, privacy and autonomy. He cautions against viewing technology merely as a human tool, and advocates considering it as an active agent.

Justin Brookman — Center for Democracy and Technology

“There are some people in the commercial space who say, ‘Oh, big data — well, let’s collect everything, keep it around forever, we’ll pay for somebody to think about security later.’ The question is whether we want to have some sort of policy framework in place to limit that.”

WIRED editorials

“What you’re about to lose is your privacy. Actually, it’s worse than that. You aren’t just going to lose your privacy — you’re going to have to watch the very concept of privacy be rewritten under your nose.”

“With IoT, you don’t need to go online — because your environment is already there, serving you.”

The closing thought of the course — ambient computing, and the provocative claim that if we never need to “go online”, IoT could dissolve the very idea of being online.

Self-check quiz

Fourteen questions. Tap an option — the correct answer fills black and an explanation appears. No marks recorded, no excuses accepted.

1 · The term “Internet of Things” was coined by —

In 1999, during a Procter and Gamble supply-chain presentation; he then co-founded MIT’s Auto-ID Center.

2 · The first international conference on the Internet of Things (IoT 2008) was held in —

IoT 2008, Zurich — also around when connected devices surpassed the human population.

3 · In the IoT protocol stack, 6LoWPAN belongs to the —

It is the adaptation layer that carries IPv6 over 802.15.4 — a network-layer function.

4 · CoAP is designed to run over —

UDP keeps the 4-byte-header protocol viable on constrained devices and lossy links.

5 · Which protocol uses a central broker with publish–subscribe topics?

MQTT: lightweight pub/sub through a broker, with QoS 0/1/2 — the telemetry standard.

6 · At IoT Level 6, the deployment template has —

Level 6 = the full stack: many nodes, cloud storage and analysis, plus a remote observer-controller.

7 · A passive RFID tag draws its operating power from —

No battery at all — energy is harvested from the reader’s field (inductive coupling or backscatter).

8 · Passive UHF RFID operates around —

860–960 MHz, far-field backscatter, 3–10 m — the supply-chain band. 13.56 MHz is HF/NFC; 125 kHz is LF.

9 · Ericsson’s forecast for connected devices by 2020 was —

50 B devices vs 7.6 B humans — roughly 6.6 devices per person. (12.5 B was the 2011 figure.)

10 · McKinsey (2015) estimated the IoT market value by 2025 at —

$11.1 T by 2025. ($14.4 T is Cisco’s two-decade net-profit figure — a classic distractor.)

11 · Which of these is NOT an IoT application-layer protocol?

TCP lives at the transport layer, below all the others.

12 · Object identifiers (Object IDs) are used to uniquely identify —

Object IDs → objects; Communication IDs → devices; Application IDs → applications and services.

13 · DDS differs from MQTT mainly because DDS is —

DDS peers talk directly over RTPS with rich real-time QoS — no central broker in the path.

14 · The four enabling technologies highlighted at WSIS 2005 were —

Identify → sense → process → shrink: the four pillars of the IoT state of the art.
