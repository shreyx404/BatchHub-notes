SECTION 01

Foundations of Probability

Experiments, events, the rules of chance, conditional probability & Bayes’ theorem

1.1 Why probability matters (the CSE lens)

In networks and systems, probability lets us predict performance and understand how delay changes with system parameters. In algorithms, randomisation produces faster, simpler algorithms than deterministic ones. In machine learning, probability is central to the underlying theory. In statistics, it is the mathematical foundation of statistical inference — the core tool of data science. Probability quantifies uncertainty and supports decision-making under uncertainty.

Probability is just a guide, not a guarantee. Toss a coin 100 times: theory says expect ≈ 50 heads, but you may get 48, or 55 — usually a number near 50. The long-run average converges; any single run does not.

Example 1.2Two marbles, with replacement

A box contains 10 white and 10 black marbles. Two marbles are drawn in succession with replacement, colour noted each time. Give S, the events “at least one of each colour” and “no white marble”, and their probabilities.

Solution

S = {bb, bw, wb, ww}. At least one of each colour = {bw, wb}; no white = {bb}.

All four outcomes equally likely: P({bw, wb}) = 2/4 = 1/2  ·  P({bb}) = 1/4.

1.3 Probability of an outcome & of an event

Definition — probability

The probability of an outcome e is a number between 0 and 1 (0 = impossible, 1 = certain). The probability of an event A is the sum of probabilities of the outcomes composing it:

P(A) = P(e₁) + P(e₂) + … + P(ek),   A = {e₁, e₂, …, ek}

Example 1.3Fair die events

A fair die is tossed. Find P(E) for E = “even number” and P(T) for T = “number greater than two”.

Solution

P(E) = 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 3/6 = 1/2  ·  P(T) = 4/6 = 2/3

Example 1.4Cards & dependent draws

(a) Find the probability of drawing a numbered card from a pack of 52. (b) There are 5 green and 7 red balls; two are drawn without replacement — find P(first green AND second red).

Solution

(a) Numbered cards = 2…10 → 9 per suit × 4 = 36.   P = 36/52 = 9/13.

(b) Without replacement the draws are dependent: P(G)·P(R|G) = (5/12)(7/11) = 35/132.

Example 1.5Working backwards from a pmf-style list

S = {a, b, c, d, e}, U = {a, b, d}, V = {b, c, d}. Given P(a) = P(b) = 0.2 and P(c) = P(d) = 0.1, find P(e), P(U), P(V).

Solution

P(e) = 1 − (0.2+0.2+0.1+0.1) = 0.4  ·  P(U) = 0.2+0.2+0.1 = 0.5  ·  P(V) = 0.2+0.1+0.1 = 0.4

1.4 The axioms & the four basic rules

The four working rules
  • Axiom 1: 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1  ·  Axiom 2: P(S) = 1
  • Axiom 3 (addition, mutually exclusive): P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)
  • General addition: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
  • Multiplication: independent → P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B);   dependent → P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B|A)

Solution

Eᶜ = “not even” = odd = {1,3,5};   Tᶜ = “not greater than two” = less than three = {1,2}.

Example 1.7Intersection — fair and loaded die

A single die is rolled. E = “even”, T = “greater than two”.
(a) Fair die: find P(E ∩ T). (b) Loaded die: P(1) = 1/12, P(6) = 3/12, the other four faces equally likely: find P(E ∩ T).

Solution

E ∩ T = {4, 6} (“even and greater than two”).

(a) P(E ∩ T) = 2/6 = 1/3.

(b) P(1) + P(6) = 4/12 = 1/3, so the remaining probability is 2/3 shared by four faces → each p = 1/6, hence P(4) = 1/6.
P(E ∩ T) = P(4) + P(6) = 1/6 + 3/12 = 2/12 + 3/12 = 5/12.

Example 1.8Union & the addition rule — job offers

P(offer from A) = 0.8, P(offer from B) = 0.6, P(both) = 0.5. Find P(at least one offer).

Solution

P(A ∪ B) = 0.8 + 0.6 − 0.5 = 0.9

Example 1.9Sensor tests — union, exactly-one, complement

120 sensors: 30 fail temperature, 25 fail pressure, 10 fail both. A sensor is chosen at random. Find (a) P(fails temperature OR pressure), (b) P(fails exactly one), (c) P(passes both).

Solution

(a) (30 + 25 − 10)/120 = 45/120 = 0.375

(b) fails temp only = 20; fails pressure only = 15 → 35/120 ≈ 0.2917

(c) complement of (a): 1 − 0.375 = 0.625

Example 1.10Three coin tosses — all operations at once

S = {hhh, hht, hth, htt, thh, tht, tth, ttt}. H = “at least one head”, M = “more heads than tails”. List H, M, H ∩ M, H ∪ M, Hᶜ with probabilities; are Hᶜ and M mutually exclusive?

Solution

H = {hhh,hht,hth,htt,thh,tht,tth} (7 outcomes) · M = {hhh,hht,hth,thh} (4)

H ∩ M = M = {hhh,hht,hth,thh}; H ∪ M = H; Hᶜ = {ttt}.

P(H ∩ M) = 4/8 · P(H ∪ M) = 7/8 · P(Hᶜ) = 1/8.

Hᶜ = {ttt} and M share no element → mutually exclusive (M ⊂ H, and Hᶜ lies entirely outside H).

1.7 Conditional probability

Definition — conditional probability
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B)

Read: “probability of A given B has already occurred.” Intuition: the given event shrinks (restricts) the sample space — the denominator becomes P(B), and only outcomes inside B matter.

Key rearrangement — the multiplication rule
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B | A)   ⟺   P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(A)

Divide when the question says “given that…” (conditional). Multiply when the question asks “probability that both…” (joint). Independence check: A, B independent ⟺ P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B).

Example 1.13Dice conditioning, both directions

A fair die is rolled. F = “a five” = {5}, O = “odd” = {1,3,5}.
(a) P(five | odd)? (b) P(odd | five)?

Solution

(a) P(F|O) = P(F ∩ O)/P(O) = (1/6)/(3/6) = 1/3 — restricted to {1,3,5}, one of three is a 5.

(b) P(O|F) = (1/6)/(1/6) = 1 — given it is a five, it is certainly odd.

Example 1.14Marbles without replacement

A bag has 5 red and 3 blue marbles (8 total). Two drawn without replacement.
(a) P(second red | first red)? (b) P(both red)? (c) In a bag of 2 blue & 3 red (5 total): P(both blue)?

Solution

(a) After removing one red: 4 red of 7 → P = 4/7 ≈ 0.571

(b) P(A ∩ B) = (5/8)(4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14 ≈ 0.357

(c) (2/5)(1/4) = 1/10

Example 1.15Conditional from a population table

60 employees: 40 male, 20 female; 12 males and 8 females know Python. Given the selected employee is male, find P(knows Python). Show both the shortcut and the formula.

Solution

Shortcut (restrict sample space): among 40 males, 12 know Python → 12/40 = 3/10.

Formula: A = male, B = knows Python. P(A) = 40/60 = 2/3; P(A ∩ B) = 12/60 = 1/5.
P(B|A) = (1/5)/(2/3) = (1/5)(3/2) = 3/10 ✓

Example 1.16Flights — conditioning & independence test

90% of flights depart on time (D), 80% arrive on time (A), 75% do both.
(a) P(A|D)? (b) P(D|A)? (c) Independent?

Solution

(a) P(A|D) = 0.75/0.9 = 0.8333

(b) P(D|A) = 0.75/0.8 = 0.9375

(c) Not independent: P(A|D) ≠ P(A), and P(A)·P(D) = 0.72 ≠ 0.75 = P(A ∩ D).

(b) Table gives P(A) = 0.3, P(R) = 0.4, P(A ∩ R) = 0.12. Independent?

Example 1.18Three-child families — conditioning practice

S = {bbb, bbg, bgb, bgg, gbb, gbg, ggb, ggg}, all equally likely. Find (1) P(at least 2 boys); (2) P(at least 2 boys | not all girls); (3) P(at least one boy); (4) P(at least one boy | first-born is a girl).

Solution

(1) {bbb,bbg,bgb,gbb} → 4/8 = 0.5

(2) Remove ggg: 4/7 ≈ 0.57

(3) 1 − P(ggg) = 7/8 = 0.875

1.8 Case study — spam detection with the axioms

Example 1.1910,000 emails

Out of 10,000 emails: 2,500 spam (S), 7,500 legitimate (L); 3,000 contain a suspicious link (A); 2,000 an unknown attachment (B); 700 both A and B; 2,100 are spam AND contain a suspicious link. Find: P(S); P(L); P(S ∪ L); P(A ∩ B); P(A ∪ B); P(S|A); are S and A independent? Are S and L mutually exclusive?

Solution

P(S) = 2500/10000 = 0.25; P(L) = 0.75; P(S) + P(L) = 1 — every email is one or the other → mutually exclusive (S ∩ L = ∅).

P(A ∩ B) = 700/10000 = 0.07. For the union: P(A) = 0.30, P(B) = 0.20, so P(A ∪ B) = 0.30 + 0.20 − 0.07 = 0.43.

P(S|A) = 2100/3000 = 0.70 — 70% of emails with a suspicious link are spam.

Independence: P(S)·P(A) = 0.25 × 0.30 = 0.075 ≠ 0.21 = P(S ∩ A) → not independent; a suspicious link strongly raises spam likelihood.

1.9 Bayes’ theorem — reversing the conditioning

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A)P(B)   with    P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|Aᶜ)P(Aᶜ)
P(A) = PRIOR (belief before) · P(B|A) = LIKELIHOOD (the evidence) · P(B) = EVIDENCE (total) · P(A|B) = POSTERIOR (revised belief)

Named after 18th-century mathematician Thomas Bayes. Bayes answers: “How should my belief change when new information arrives?” — cloudy sky → rain chance rises; smoke seen → fire chance rises; good internal marks → pass chance rises.

Exam tip — solving Bayes numerals

1) Write what is asked (the reversed conditional). 2) Spot what is given — the forward conditional P(B|A) and the base rates P(A), P(B). 3) Substitute. The event that “happens first” usually supplies the prior.

Example 1.20Pain clinic

10% of patients are prescribed narcotic pain killers (A); 5% of all patients are addicts (B); of those prescribed pain pills, 8% are addicts. If a patient is an addict, what is the probability they are prescribed pain pills?

Solution

Asked: P(A|B). Given: P(B|A) = 0.08, P(A) = 0.10, P(B) = 0.05.

P(A|B) = (0.08 × 0.10) / 0.05 = 0.16

A 16% chance an addict is prescribed pain pills.

Example 1.21Liver disease

P(liver disease) = 10%; P(alcoholic) = 5%; among liver-disease patients, 7% are alcoholics. If a patient is alcoholic, P(liver disease)?

Solution

P(A|B) = (0.07 × 0.10)/0.05 = 0.14 — a 14% chance.

Example 1.22Factory quality test (count form of Bayes)

1000 sensors: 100 defective (D), of which 80 fail the quality test (F); of the 900 good sensors, 90 also fail F. A sensor failed the test — probability it is actually defective?

Solution

Failures total = 80 + 90 = 170, of which 80 are defective:

P(D | F) = 80 / 170 ≈ 0.4706
Example 1.23Bot detection (with total probability)

A = “user is a bot” (10% of users); B = “fails CAPTCHA”. Bots fail CAPTCHA 80% of the time; humans fail 10% of the time. Find P(A|B).

Solution

P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|Aᶜ)P(Aᶜ) = 0.80(0.10) + 0.10(0.90) = 0.08 + 0.09 = 0.17

P(A|B) = 0.08 / 0.17 ≈ 0.471

Failing CAPTCHA raises bot-probability from 10% to about 47.1%.

Key idea — where Bayes runs the modern world
  • Naïve Bayes classifier (one of the first ML algorithms): words → spam; review words → sentiment; traffic features → attack/normal.
  • Sequential updating — robot navigation: sensor → update → camera → update → GPS → update (sensor fusion).
  • Bayesian networks: Rain → Wet road → Traffic → Late arrival (expert systems, medical & fault diagnosis, AI reasoning).
  • Bayesian inference: treat parameters themselves as uncertain — prior distribution → posterior distribution → credible intervals (A/B testing, reliability).
  • Research frontier: MCMC, Gibbs sampling, variational inference, Bayesian optimisation, Hidden Markov Models, Kalman & particle filters, Bayesian deep learning — behind self-driving cars, robotics, LLMs, weather and financial forecasting.
SECTION 02

Random Variables

Turning uncertain outcomes into numbers — the foundation of every distribution

2.1 What is a random variable?

Definition

A random variable (RV) is a real-valued function whose domain is the sample space of a random experiment:  X : S → ℝ. Each outcome ω ∈ S is assigned a single real number X(ω) = f(ω) — a quantity that depends on chance.

Think of it as a translator: computers prefer numbers, so Head → 1, Tail → 0. Sample-space outcomes like “red ball” can’t be averaged or integrated — random variables make arithmetic possible (averages, spread, probabilities of ranges).

Experiment Random variable X Possible values
Toss a coin once Result (H=1, T=0) 0, 1
Roll a die Face shown 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6
Toss a coin twice Number of heads 0, 1, 2
Students absent today Count of absentees 0, 1, 2, 3, …
Waiting time at ATM Minutes waited 2.5, 3.12, 6.78, 12.01, … (measured)

Example 2.4Classification drill

Classify: (a) students absent in a class (b) CPU temperature (c) packets lost during transfer (d) time to download 2 GB (e) defective LEDs in a batch (f) cyberattacks per day (g) battery voltage (h) vehicles passing a toll booth (i) rainfall in a day (j) compile time of a C++ program.

Key idea — why “random”?

“How many students will be absent tomorrow?” Nobody knows today — 4, 12 or 8. The value is uncertain, hence random; once attendance is taken, it becomes fixed. The AI canteen asks “how many students will buy lunch?”, “how long will they queue?” — the first is a count (discrete), the second a measurement (continuous).

2.3 The pipeline — from uncertainty to engineering decisions

real uncertain quantity→random variable X→probability distribution→E[X] & Var(X)→engineering decision

Example: server requests → X = requests/minute → Poisson distribution → probability of heavy traffic → decide server capacity. This is where random variables stop being definitions and start being engineering.

SECTION 03

PMF · PDF · CDF

The three ways a distribution speaks — mass, density, and the running total

3.1 What a probability distribution is

A probability distribution describes how probability is distributed among the possible values of a random variable. A random variable tells us what can happen; the distribution tells us how likely each possibility is. It can be represented as a table, a graph, or a mathematical formula. For a fair die, each face carries probability 1/6. A probability distribution is also the idealised frequency distribution of a data-generating process — the foundation for confidence intervals and hypothesis testing.

3.2 Probability Mass Function (PMF) — discrete

Definition — PMF
p(x) = P(X = x)

Properties of a valid PMF:   (i) p(x) ≥ 0 for all x;   (ii) Σx∈S p(x) = 1.   For any set A: P(X ∈ A) = Σx∈A p(x).

For a discrete probability distribution, we list every value with its probability. The height of each bar in the PMF graph is the probability of that outcome.

Example 3.1Three fair coins

A fair coin is flipped 3 times; X = number of heads. Build the PMF.

Solution

S = {HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT}

P(X=0) = 1/8 · P(X=1) = 3/8 · P(X=2) = 3/8 · P(X=3) = 1/8   (sum = 1 ✓)

Example 3.2Larger of two dice

Roll a red and a green die; X = the larger of the two numbers (the common value if equal). Out of 36 equally likely points, find the PMF.

Solution

P(X=1)=1/36, P(X=2)=3/36, P(X=3)=5/36, P(X=4)=7/36, P(X=5)=9/36, P(X=6)=11/36 — i.e.

P(X = x) = (2x − 1)/36,   x = 1, …, 6

Why: outcomes with both dice ≤ x number x²; ≤ (x−1) number (x−1)²; the difference 2x − 1 gives max exactly x.

Example 3.3Finding the normalising constant

(a) f(x) = bx³ for x = 1, 2, 3 — find b. (b) f(x) = k(7x + 3) for x = 1, 2, 3 (0 otherwise) — find k.

Solution

(a) b(1³ + 2³ + 3³) = 1 → b(36) = 1 → b = 1/36

(b) k(7·1+3) + k(7·2+3) + k(7·3+3) = k(10 + 17 + 24) = 51k = 1 → k = 1/51

Example 3.4Solving a quadratic for k

X has PMF: P(X=0) = 2k² + k, P(X=1) = 6k, P(X=2) = 8k² + 2k (values arranged so the total is 10k² + 9k). Find k.

Solution

Σf(x) = 1 → 2k² + k + 6k + 8k² + 2k = 1 → 10k² + 9k − 1 = 0

Factorise: 10k² + 10k − k − 1 = 10k(k+1) − 1(k+1) = (10k − 1)(k + 1) = 0 → k = 0.1 or k = −1

Probabilities must lie in [0,1] → reject −1 → k = 0.1

Example 3.5Rapid-fire PMF exercises

(a) P(X=x) = x/15, x ∈ {1,…,5}: find P(X ≤ 3). (b) P(X=x) = x²/30, x ∈ {1,…,4}: find P(X ≤ 2). (c) Same as (a): find P(X < 4). (d) The distribution below: find P(X > 4).

x 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
P(X=x) 0.10 0.15 0.20 0.25 0.15 0.10 0.04 0.01
Solution

(a) (1+2+3)/15 = 6/15 = 0.4   (b) (1+4)/30 = 5/30 = 1/6   (c) (1+2+3)/15 = 0.4   (d) 0.10 + 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.15

3.3 Probability Density Function (PDF) — continuous

Definition — PDF
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab f(x) dx

Properties: (i) f(x) ≥ 0; (ii) ∫−∞∞ f(x) dx = 1 (total area = 1); (iii) P(X = a) = 0 for any single point — so endpoints don’t matter: P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(a < X < b); (iv) f(x) is a density, not a probability — it may exceed 1; only the area must equal 1.

Since there are uncountably many possible values, a table is impossible — we draw a smooth curve. Think of a bell-shaped mountain: the curve shows where values are likely and where they are not. Probability of waiting between 4 and 6 minutes, or temperature between 25 °C and 30 °C, is an area under the curve.

Example 3.6Uniform on [0, 2]

f(x) = 1/2 for 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, 0 otherwise. Verify and compute P(0.5 ≤ X ≤ 1.5).

Solution

3.4 Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) — for both types

Definition — CDF
F(x) = P(X ≤ x)

Properties (any RV): (i) 0 ≤ F(x) ≤ 1; (ii) non-decreasing (“going upstairs” — if your F ever decreases, you have made an error); (iii) F → 0 as x → −∞ and F → 1 as x → +∞; (iv) right-continuous (jumps at each value of a discrete X); (v) P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a).

Links: discrete → F(x) = Σk ≤ x p(k) (a staircase that jumps by p(x));   continuous → F(x) = ∫−∞x f(t) dt, and f(x) = dF(x)/dx wherever differentiable. CDFs are also used to compute p-values in hypothesis testing.

Example 3.15Concept check — which tool, and why

(a) Waiting time to find parking — discrete or continuous? PMF or PDF? Can P(exactly 5.000 min) be computed? Is P(4 ≤ X ≤ 6) more meaningful? SECTION 04

Expectation & Variance

Where is the centre? How far do values wander from it?

4.1 Expectation — the long-run average

E[X] = Σx x · p(x)   (discrete)    ·    E[X] = ∫−∞∞ x · f(x) dx   (continuous)
A weighted average of possible values, weighted by probability. Describes the long-term average level; measures the centre of the distribution.

Rolling a die: E[X] = (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.5 — you can never roll a 3.5, yet over 1000 rolls the average per roll converges to 3.5.

Example 4.1Three coins

X = heads in 3 tosses (PMF 1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8). Find E[X].

Solution

E[X] = 0(1/8) + 1(3/8) + 2(3/8) + 3(1/8) = 3/2

Example 4.2Fabric imperfections

Imperfections per 10 m of fabric: x = 0…4 with f(x) = 0.41, 0.37, 0.16, 0.05, 0.01. Find the average number of imperfections.

Solution

E[X] = 0(0.41) + 1(0.37) + 2(0.16) + 3(0.05) + 4(0.01) = 0.37+0.32+0.15+0.04 = 0.88

Example 4.3Decision problems — is it worth it?

(a) A club invests ₹10,000 in a football game; expects ₹15,000 ticket sales unless it rains (20% chance), in which case it loses everything. Expected return? (b) A stock yields +$4000 with p = 0.3 and −$1000 with p = 0.7 — expected gain? (c) A gadget sells at +₹30 profit if working (49/50) but −₹800 if faulty (1/50) — profitable long-term?

Solution

(a) E(X) = 5000(0.8) − 10000(0.2) = 4000 − 2000 = ₹2000 — a good investment on average.

(b) E(X) = 4000(0.3) − 1000(0.7) = $500

(c) E(X) = 30(49/50) − 800(1/50) = 29.4 − 16 = ₹13.4 per gadget — yes, profitable.

Example 4.4Biased coin

A head is three times as likely as a tail. Find the expected number of tails in two tosses.

Solution

3P(T) + P(T) = 1 → P(T) = 1/4, P(H) = 3/4.

X = #tails: P(0) = (3/4)² = 9/16, P(1) = 2(3/4)(1/4) = 6/16, P(2) = 1/16.

E[X] = 0 + 6/16 + 2/16 = 0.5

Example 4.5The class-size paradox — weighting matters

A school has 3 classes of 5, 10 and 150 students. (a) Pick a class at random, X = its size: E[X]? (b) Pick a student at random, Y = size of their class: E[Y]? Why do they differ?

Solution

(a) E[X] = (5+10+150)/3 = 55

(b) E[Y] = 5(5/165) + 10(10/165) + 150(150/165) = 22635/165 ≈ 137

Big classes contain many students, so they dominate the student-weighted average. Same data, different weighting → different expectation.

Example 4.6Continuous expectations

(a) Elevator waiting time: f(x) = x for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, 2 − x for 1 < x ≤ 2. Find E[X]. (b) A dealer’s profit X (in units of $5000) has density f(x) = 2(1 − x) on (0, 1). Average profit per automobile?

Solution

(a) E[X] = ∫₀¹ x·x dx + ∫₁² x(2−x) dx = [x³/3]₀¹ + [x² − x³/3]₁² = 1/3 + (4 − 8/3) − (1 − 1/3) = 1/3 + 4/3 − 2/3 = 1

(b) E[X] = ∫₀¹ 2x(1−x) dx = 2[x²/2 − x³/3]₀¹ = 2(1/2 − 1/3) = 1/3 → average profit = 5000/3 ≈ $1667

Example 4.7Airline complaints

Complaints per day: x = 0…8 with probabilities 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.15, 0.21, 0.13, 0.10, 0.07, 0.04. (a) Expected complaints per day? (b) P(complaints exceed the expected number)?

(b) Exceeding 3.75 means X ≥ 4: 0.21+0.13+0.10+0.07+0.04 = 0.55

4.2 LOTUS — expectation of a function of X

Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS)
E[g(X)] = Σx g(x)·p(x)   ·   E[g(X)] = ∫ g(x)·f(x) dx

To find the average of a function of X (e.g. winnings = roll²), you do not need the distribution of g(X) — just plug g(x) into the original probabilities. The name jokes that statisticians use it “unconsciously”, assuming it is obvious; it is a genuine theorem and a brilliant shortcut when deriving new distributions is a nightmare (or impossible).

Example 4.8Carnival game

A fair 3-sided die rolls X ∈ {1,2,3} with probability 1/3 each. The prize is the square of the roll. Find the expected payout — the hard way and the LOTUS way.

Solution

Hard way: build the distribution of Y = X²: P(Y=1)=P(Y=4)=P(Y=9)=1/3, then E[Y] = (1+4+9)/3.

LOTUS way: E[X²] = 1²(1/3) + 2²(1/3) + 3²(1/3) = 14/3 ≈ 4.67 — use the original probabilities directly, no new table.

(a) Algorithm A never takes more time than B (X ≤ Y); E[X] = 5 ms. Could E[Y] = 4 ms? (b) Process A’s cost is always ≤ Process B’s; E[X] = ₹40, E[Y] = ₹55 — consistent?

4.4 Variance & standard deviation — why the mean is not enough

Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²]   ·   shortcut: Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²   ·   SD(X) = σ = √Var(X)
Low variance → outcomes cluster near the mean · High variance → wide scatter · SD shares the units of X (variance is in squared units).
Example 4.11Fair die — full calculation

Find Var(X) and SD(X) for a fair die.

Solution

E[X] = 3.5;   E[X²] = (1+4+9+16+25+36)/6 = 91/6 ≈ 15.17

Var(X) = 91/6 − (3.5)² = 15.17 − 12.25 = 2.917  ·  SD ≈ 1.71

Example 4.12Practice pair

(a) P(X=1)=0.2, P(X=2)=0.3, P(X=3)=0.5 — find E[X] and Var(X). (b) Given E[X] = 4, Var(X) = 9 — find E[3X−2] and Var(3X−2).

Solution

(a) E[X] = 1(.2)+2(.3)+3(.5) = 2.3;   E[X²] = 1(.2)+4(.3)+9(.5) = 5.9;   Var = 5.9 − 2.3² = 5.9 − 5.29 = 0.61

(b) E[3X−2] = 3(4)−2 = 10;   Var(3X−2) = 3²·Var(X) = 9 × 9 = 81

4.5 Properties of variance

Property Statement
Non-negativity Var(X) ≥ 0 always
Shift invariance Var(X + b) = Var(X) — adding a constant never changes spread
Scaling Var(aX) = a²·Var(X) — hence Var(aX + b) = a²·Var(X) (b contributes nothing!)
Constant Var(c) = 0
Independent sums Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) — and even Var(X − Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)!
General (dependent) case Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2·Cov(X,Y),   Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y]
Common mistakes
  • Writing Var(aX + b) = a²Var(X) + b — wrong: the constant b never affects spread.
  • Assuming Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) without independence — only E is always additive.
  • Forgetting E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] requires independence.
SECTION 05

Bernoulli Distribution

One trial, two outcomes — the atom from which Binomial is built

5.1 Definition

Definition — Bernoulli(p)

The simplest discrete distribution: a single experiment with exactly two outcomes — success (X = 1) with probability p, failure (X = 0) with probability 1 − p, where 0 < p < 1.

P(X = x) = px(1 − p)1−x,   x ∈ {0, 1}
E[X] = p   ·   Var(X) = p(1 − p)

Derivations: E[X] = 1·p + 0·(1−p) = p;   E[X²] = 1²·p + 0²·(1−p) = p, so Var = p − p² = p(1−p).

Examples: one coin toss (H = success, p = 1/2); pass/fail test; a single factory part defective or working; a transmitted bit received correctly or in error; a loan approved or not; fraud / not fraud; sensor ON/OFF.

5.2 Worked examples

Example 5.1Microchip defect (p = 0.08)

A chip is defective with probability 8%. X = 1 if defective, 0 otherwise. Write the PMF; find E[X] and Var(X).

Solution

PMF: P(1) = 0.08, P(0) = 0.92  ·  E[X] = 0.08  ·  Var(X) = 0.08 × 0.92 = 0.0736

Example 5.2Medical screening (p = 0.92)

A test correctly detects the virus with 92% accuracy on a single patient. (a) P(test fails)? (b) Var(X)?

Solution

(a) 0.08   (b) 0.92 × 0.08 = 0.0736 — same variance as Example 5.1 by the symmetry p ↔ 1−p.

Example 5.3Basketball free throw (p = 0.75)

A player’s historic free-throw accuracy is 75%. For one attempt: expected value and standard deviation.

Solution

E[X] = 0.75; Var = 0.75(0.25) = 0.1875; SD = √0.1875 ≈ 0.433

5.3 Applications — Bernoulli inside machine learning

  • Binary targets: spam (1) vs not (0); disease present vs healthy; churn vs stay — the label itself is a Bernoulli variable.
  • Training: logistic regression / sigmoid neural nets output a predicted probability p̂ of class 1; the Binary Cross-Entropy (log) loss is the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli distribution.
  • Bernoulli Naïve Bayes: models presence/absence of each word (1/0) as independent Bernoulli trials — exceptionally fast for spam filtering and sentiment analysis.
  • Engineering: quality control (item defective or not), digital communication (bit error or not), component stress testing, a single server request succeeding or timing out.
SECTION 06

Binomial Distribution

Counting successes in a fixed number of yes/no trials — James Bernoulli, 1738

6.1 The idea

Many real-life and business situations are pass–fail: heads or tails, win or lose, defective or not, spam or not. The prefix “bi” means two — exactly two possible outcomes. The Binomial distribution gives the probability of obtaining exactly x successes in N Bernoulli trials; the underlying process is the Bernoulli process. It answers: “What is the probability of exactly x successes in n independent trials?”

Typical problems: defective/non-defective items in a production run; yes/no surveys (150 people asked if they watch ABP news); vote counts for a candidate; successful sales calls; male/female workers in a company; students passing an exam; cybersecurity login attempts; IoT packet transmission success; clinical trials.

Note: “success” simply means the event you are interested in counting — it needn’t be anything good (a defective item is a “success” when counting defectives!).

6.2 When may you use it? — BINS / FTPI

Memory device B I N S  =  F T P I Binary — only two outcomes per trial (answers / doesn’t answer) · Independent — one trial never affects another · Number — fixed number of trials n (exactly 10, not 9, not 12) · Success — same probability p on every trial  ⟺  Fixed trials · Two outcomes · Probability constant · Independent trials.   See FTPI / BINS → think Binomial.

6.3 The formula — and why the combination appears

P(X = x) = (nx) · px · qn−x,    x = 0, 1, 2, …, n
with   C(n, x) = n!x!(n−x)!  ·  p = P(success)  ·  q = 1 − p  ·  n = number of trials  ·  x = number of successes
READ AS:  (number of arrangements) × (probability of the x successes) × (probability of the n−x failures)
Key idea — why you can’t just multiply

Three customers called, we want exactly two to answer. Is the answer 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.3? No — that is only ONE arrangement (A A N). There are three arrangements: AAN, ANA, NAA. Each has probability 0.7² × 0.3, so the total is C(3,2) × 0.7² × 0.3. The combination term exists precisely to count all the ways the successes can be scattered among the trials.

6.4 Moments, CDF and shape

Summary statistics
E[X] = np
Var(X) = np(1 − p)
SD = √(npq)
Mode = ⌊(n+1)p⌋  (two modes if (n+1)p is an integer)
CDF & tail tricks
F(x) = Σk ≤ x C(n,k) pk qn−k
P(X ≥ 1) = 1 − qn
“At least one success” = complement of “all failures”.
Shape
Skewness γ₁ = (1−2p)/√(npq)
Excess kurtosis = (1−6pq)/(npq)
Symmetric at p = 0.5 · right-skewed p < 0.5 · left-skewed p > 0.5 · approaches Normal when np ≥ 5 and nq ≥ 5.

(a) Fair coin tossed 20 times, X = heads: E[X], Var(X)? (b) 10 students answer a True/False question, each correct with p = 0.8: expected number correct? (c) A fair coin flipped 10 times: mean, variance, SD — interpret.

Solution

(a) E[X] = 20(0.5) = 10; Var = 20(0.5)(0.5) = 5

(b) E[X] = np = 10(0.8) = 8

(c) E = 5 (half the flips “should” be heads); Var = 2.5 → SD ≈ 1.58 — a small, expected spread: most results fall within about 5 ± 1.58 heads.

Example 6.12ML model evaluation — chance performance

A classifier has baseline accuracy 90% (p = 0.9). Tested on n = 100 items, what is the probability of exactly 85 correct purely by chance?

Solution
P(X=85) = C(100,85)(0.9)⁸⁵(0.1)¹⁵ ≈ 0.0331

Only ≈ 3.31% — seeing 85/100 would be genuinely surprising under the baseline; this idea underlies confidence intervals for model metrics.

Example 6.13Application narratives

(a) A cancer therapy cures 10% of patients; 500 patients take it. Expected recoveries? If 75 respond, what does Binomial tell us? (b) 4% of all emails are spam; an account gets 20 emails — P(exactly 2 spam)?

Solution

(a) Expected recoveries = np = 500(0.10) = 50. Binomial computes P(X ≥ 75) — vanishingly small under p = 0.1, questioning whether the observed 75 reflects a better-than-expected therapy.

(b) C(20,2)(0.04)²(0.96)¹⁸ = 190 × 0.0016 × 0.4796 ≈ 0.146

When NOT to use Binomial

“How many customers visit a shop in one hour?” · “How many calls arrive per minute?” · “How many emails today?” — there is no fixed number of trials; events occur randomly over time. These belong to the Poisson distribution (Section 07).

Rapid identification checklist

Fixed trials? ✔ Two outcomes? ✔ Constant p? ✔ Independent? ✔ — all four YES → Binomial. Random Experiment → Fixed Number of Trials? → Two Outcomes Only? → Probability Constant? → Independent Trials? → USE BINOMIAL.

SECTION 07

Poisson Distribution

The law of rare events — counting randomness per unit of time, space, area or volume

7.1 The idea

French mathematician Siméon-Denis Poisson developed his function in 1830 to describe how often a gambler would win a rarely-won game of chance over many tries. The Poisson (or “count”) distribution models the number of times a rare/random event occurs within a fixed unit of time, area, volume or length: cases of a disease in different towns, mutations in chromosome regions, particles emitted by a radioactive source, births per hour — and whether such events occur randomly in time or space.

Applications catalogue (memorise a few)

mutations on a DNA strand per time unit · bankruptcies filed per month · car-wash arrivals per hour · network failures per day · file-server virus infections per 24 h · asthma arrivals per hour at a walk-in clinic · persons entering a restaurant per day · births, deaths, marriages, divorces, suicides, homicides over a period · service complaints per month · website visitors per minute.

7.2 The law of rare events — Binomial’s limit

Poisson as a limit of Binomial

Poisson models the limit of a Binomial distribution when the number of trials is very large but the probability of success very small — n → ∞, p → 0, with the average λ = np held constant. Use the approximation when n is large (≥ 100) and p is small (≤ 0.05).

Real-world intuition: counting web traffic per minute, you could split a minute into 60 seconds (Binomial with n = 60). But visitors can land at any millisecond — as you break time into infinitely tiny intervals (n → ∞), the chance of a click in any one micro-interval drops to near zero (p → 0) while the average rate per minute (λ) stays fixed. That continuous framework is Poisson.

7.3 Conditions for Poisson

  1. An event can occur any number of times during a time period.
  2. Events occur independently — one occurrence does not affect another.
  3. The rate of occurrence is constant — it does not change with time.
  4. The probability of an event is proportional to the length of the period (10 calls in 1 hr → 20 calls in 2 hr).
  5. Events cannot occur twice at exactly the same instant — some interval, however small, separates them.

7.4 The formula

P(X = x) = e−λ λxx!,    x = 0, 1, 2, 3, …
e = Euler’s number ≈ 2.71828 · λ > 0 is the average rate (expected value) · λt version: P(x; λt) = e−λt(λt)x/x! for a period of length t
READ AS: “probability of exactly x events when the average is λ.”   ⬤   E[X] = λ  ·  Var(X) = λ — mean = variance!
Situation λ
Average calls per minute = 4 λ = 4
Average emails per hour = 20 λ = 20
Average cyber-attacks per hour = 7 λ = 7

x is simply how many events actually occurred (0, 1, 2, 3, …) — possible values continue forever; there is no fixed n.

7.5 The five-step method

1 · identify the event→2 · find the average λ→3 · find required count x→4 · substitute P = e−λλx/x!→5 · interpret in context

Step 5 matters: if you compute P(X = 5) = 0.18, say “there is an 18% probability of exactly 5 calls in the next minute” — that interpretation is what turns arithmetic into an engineering decision.

Example 7.1Storms (λt form)

Average 2 major storms per year. P(exactly 3 storms next year)?

Solution

λ = 2, t = 1, x = 3, e = 2.71828:

P(3) = e−2·2³/3! = (0.13534)(8)/6 = 0.180
Example 7.2Radioactive counter

Mean 4 particles per millisecond. P(6 particles in a given millisecond)?

Solution

Example 7.3Warehouse demand — zero events

Demand averages 5 times per day. P(no request at all on a given day)?

Solution

Example 7.4Binomial → Poisson switch (λ = np)

1 in 1000 transistors is defective. Find P(4 defectives in a batch of 2000).

Solution

P(4) = e−2·2⁴/4! = 0.13534 × 16/24 ≈ 0.0902
Example 7.5Traffic accidents — three question shapes

3 accidents per month on average at an intersection. Find (a) P(exactly 5), (b) P(fewer than 3), (c) P(at least 2).

Solution

λ = 3, e−3 = 0.049787.

(a) P(5) = e−33⁵/5! = 0.049787 × 243/120 ≈ 0.1008

(b) P(X < 3) = P(0)+P(1)+P(2) = e−3(1 + 3 + 9/2) = 0.049787 × 8.5 ≈ 0.4232

(c) P(X ≥ 2) = 1 − P(0) − P(1) = 1 − 0.0498 − 0.1494 ≈ 0.8009

Example 7.6Café arrivals & a pmf value

(a) Customers arrive at mean rate 2 per minute — P(5 arrivals in 1 minute)? (b) Find the mass function at x = 6 if the mean is 3.4.

Solution

(a) P(5) = e−22⁵/5! = 0.13534 × 32/120 ≈ 0.036 — a 3.6% chance.

(b) P(6) = e−3.43.4⁶/6! = 0.03337 × 1544.8/720 ≈ 0.072

Example 7.7Cumulative Poisson — “less than 2”

3% of electronic units are defective. In a sample of 200, find P(fewer than 2 defective).

Solution

p small, n large → Poisson: λ = np = 200 × 0.03 = 6.

Example 7.8Lions on safari — four-term sum

5 lions seen per 1-day safari on average. P(tourists see fewer than 4 lions)?

Solution

λ = 5, x = 0, 1, 2, 3; e−5 = 0.006738:

P = 0.006738[1 + 5 + 25/2 + 125/6] = 0.0067 + 0.0337 + 0.0842 + 0.1404 ≈ 0.2650

The probability of seeing no more than 3 lions is 0.2650.

Example 7.9Security & defects — quick substitutions

(a) Firewall receives 2 intrusion attempts per minute — P(exactly 1)? (b) Factory observes 3 defective chips per circuit board — P(exactly 2)?

Solution

(a) λ = 2, x = 1: P = e−2·2 = 0.13534 × 2 = 0.2707

(b) λ = 3, x = 2: P = e−3·9/2 = 0.049787 × 4.5 ≈ 0.224

Example 7.10Recognising the setting (conceptual)

Classify and set up (no computation): (i) a bank receives 3 customers/minute — “how many in the next minute?”; (ii) a website sees 5 hacking attempts/hour — “how many next hour?”; (iii) potholes along a road — P(exactly 5 in the next 10 km); (iv) accidents on a 20 km stretch per month; (v) trees in the next 1 km; (vi) cell towers in the next 25 km; (vii) Wi-Fi access points in 1 km²; (viii) dead pixels in 100 cm²; (ix) defective chips on a 50 cm² wafer.

Solution

All are Poisson: each counts random events over an interval of time (i, ii, iv) or space/area (iii, v, vi, vii, viii, ix) with no fixed number of trials. Only λ (average per unit) and x are needed.

SECTION 08

Uniform Distribution

Every value in a range equally likely — probability becomes length

8.1 The idea

Definition

A random value occurs anywhere between two limits, and every value in that range is equally likely: a server generating a random number between 0 and 10 gives 2, 5.3, 5.12, 8.785, 9.5 — all with the same density. No part of the range is preferred. Uniform models situations with no reason to favour one value over another.

Two types: discrete uniform — a finite set of equally likely values (fair die: P = 1/6 each); continuous uniform — X ∼ U(a, b), any value in [a, b] with equal density. In engineering and statistics, “Uniform” means the continuous one unless stated otherwise.

8.2 Formulas

PDF
f(x) = 1/(b − a)   for a ≤ x ≤ b;   0 otherwise
X ∼ U(10, 20) → f(x) = 1/10 = 0.1
CDF
F(x) = 0 (x < a);   (x − a)/(b − a) (a ≤ x ≤ b);   1 (x > b)
Rises linearly from 0 to 1 across the interval.
Moments
E[X] = (a + b)/2 — the midpoint
Var(X) = (b − a)²/12  ·  σ = (b − a)/√12
U(10, 30): mean 20; Var = 400/12 ≈ 33.33; σ ≈ 5.77

8.3 The master shortcut

Master trick Probability = favorable interval ÷ total interval P(c ≤ X ≤ d) = (d − c)/(b − a).   Imagine a ruler from a to b — every section equally likely. “What is P(landing between c and d)?” = “How long is my piece compared to the whole ruler?” That single idea solves most Uniform problems. Note the contrast: Binomial → how many successes? · Poisson → how many events? · Uniform → which value within a range?
Example 8.1Shortcut drills

(a) Response time ∼ U(2, 8): P(3 ≤ X ≤ 5)? (b) Sensor delay ∼ U(10, 50) ms: P(X > 30)? (c) X ∼ U(100, 200): P(X < 130)? (d) Packet delay ∼ U(20, 80) ms: P(35 < X < 50)? (e) X ∼ U(20, 100): P(40 < X < 70)?

Solution

(a) (5−3)/(8−2) = 2/6 = 0.3333   (b) (50−30)/(50−10) = 20/40 = 0.5 (30 is the midpoint — intuitive!)   (c) (130−100)/100 = 0.3   (d) (50−35)/(80−20) = 15/60 = 0.25   (e) (70−40)/(100−20) = 30/80 = 0.375

8.4 The continuous trap — never count, always measure

Common mistake — discrete counting on a continuous variable

For a continuous variable, individual points have probability zero and there are infinitely many real numbers between any two values. Counting integers distorts the true proportion (it ignores all the decimals). Discrete variable → count outcomes; continuous variable → measure interval length. Endpoints never matter: P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(a < X < b).

Example 8.2Tricky questions

(a) RNG uniform on [0, 100]: P(X > 60)? (b) RNG uniform on [0, 10]: P(X > 7.5)? (c) Uniform on [0, 1]: P(X > 0.999)?

Solution

(a) (100−60)/100 = 0.4 — the counting answer “40 numbers out of 100” coincidentally matches, but the correct reasoning is interval length.

(b) Length: (10−7.5)/10 = 0.25. The tempting count “8, 9, 10 → 3/10 = 0.3” is wrong — X is continuous; infinitely many values fill (7.5, 10].

(c) (1−0.999)/1 = 0.001 — not 1/100; uncountably many values exceed 0.999.

Example 8.3Endpoints & the counting trap, quantified

Latency in a cloud load-balancer is uniform on [10, 30] ms. A QoS alert triggers in the degraded window 22 ≤ X ≤ 26.
(a) Correct continuous probability. (b) The probability a student would wrongly get by counting integer latencies. (c) What is P(X = 22 or X = 26)?

Solution

(a) (26−22)/(30−10) = 4/20 = 0.20

(b) Integers 22…26 = 5 states of 21 total (10…30) → 5/21 ≈ 0.238 — wrong, because the 21 integer states are not equally likely representatives of a continuum.

(c) 0 — single points carry zero probability, so including or excluding endpoints changes nothing.

Example 8.4Alarm design

A machine generates temperature uniformly in [15, 35] °C. An alarm sounds when 18 ≤ T ≤ 24. (a) Correct probability; (b) the mistaken integer-counting result.

Solution

(a) (24−18)/(35−15) = 6/20 = 0.30   (b) integers 18…24 = 7 of 21 → ≈ 0.333 — overstates the risk.

SECTION 09

Exponential Distribution

The waiting time until the next event — Poisson’s continuous twin

9.1 The idea

Definition — Exponential(λ)

The Exponential distribution naturally models waiting time: time until the next request, the next failure, the next attack, the next packet, the next customer call. If a server receives requests randomly, “how long until the next request arrives?” is a typical Exponential problem. λ is the rate parameter — how frequently events occur (λ = 5 means 5 events per unit time).

Random independent arrivals it models: radioactive decay emissions (one atom’s decay says nothing about the next); photons striking a sensor; web-server HTTP requests (one user’s click doesn’t trigger another’s); network packet arrivals at a router; uncorrelated database queries; emergency-room patients; call-centre calls; ATM visits. Also: time until a server fails, time between sensor events, time until a machine component fails.

9.2 Formulas

PDF
f(x) = λe−λx,   x ≥ 0
Density is highest at x = 0 and decays: short waits likely, very long waits unlikely.
CDF pair — the workhorses
P(X ≤ x) = 1 − e−λx
P(X > x) = e−λx (survival)
LESS → 1 − exponential · MORE → exponential.
Moments
E[X] = 1/λ  ·  Var(X) = 1/λ²  ·  σ = 1/λ
Special property: mean = SD = 1/λ. Rate ↔ time duality: 5 requests/s → average wait 0.2 s.
Memoryless property
P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t)
The future wait does not depend on how long you have already waited.
Example 9.1Next request within 1 second

A web server receives λ = 2 requests/second. P(next request arrives within 1 second)?

Solution

P(X ≤ 1) = 1 − e−2(1) = 1 − e−2 = 1 − 0.1353 = 0.8647 — 86.47%.

Sanity check: average wait = 1/λ = 0.5 s, so waiting under 1 s should indeed be likely.

Example 9.2Cyber incidents — within 30 minutes

A monitoring system detects λ = 3 incidents/hour. P(next incident within 30 minutes)?

Solution

Convert units: 30 min = 0.5 h.   P(X ≤ 0.5) = 1 − e−3(0.5) = 1 − e−1.5 = 1 − 0.2231 ≈ 0.7769

Example 9.3Survival — no incident for an hour

Same system (λ = 3/hr). P(no incident for the next 1 hour)?

Solution

P(X > 1) = e−3(1) = e−3 ≈ 0.0498 — only a 4.98% chance of waiting more than an hour.

Example 9.4Cloud server failures (λ = 0.2/hr)

Failures occur at 0.2/hour (1 per 5 h). Find (a) mean time between failures; (b) P(next failure within 2 h); (c) P(survives 10 h without failure).

Solution

(a) E[X] = 1/0.2 = 5 hours

(b) P(X ≤ 2) = 1 − e−0.2(2) = 1 − e−0.4 ≈ 0.3297 (32.97%)

(c) P(X > 10) = e−0.2(10) = e−2 ≈ 0.1353 (13.53%)

9.3 The memoryless property

Key idea — no memory
P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t)

You have already waited 10 minutes for a server request — is it now “due”? No. For an exponential model, the probability of waiting another 2 seconds is exactly what it was at the start. A request that hasn’t arrived for 5 seconds is not “surely coming soon” — the future wait is independent of the past. This is precisely why Exponential suits random, independent arrivals.

9.4 Poisson ↔ Exponential — the perfect pair

With λ = 6 calls/hour: “How many calls arrive in the next hour?” → Poisson (discrete count). “How long until the next call?” → Exponential (continuous time). Recognising the phrasing — “events occur at an average rate λ; find the probability you have to wait…” → instantly Exponential, with P(X ≤ t) = 1 − e−λt. The Poisson–Exponential connection is one of the most useful ideas in probability for CSE.

Exam formula sheet — Exponential

11.4 Common exam mistakes — final warnings

Don’t lose marks here
  • Using P(A) + P(B) for a union without subtracting the overlap.
  • Calling mutually exclusive events “independent” — they are different concepts.
  • Forgetting the C(n,x) arrangement factor in Binomial (counting only one ordering).
  • Forgetting qn−x, or mixing up x and n in exponents.
  • Using a PDF value as a probability (densities can exceed 1), or claiming P(X = a) > 0 for continuous X.
  • Counting integers on a Uniform (continuous) problem.
  • Writing Var(aX + b) = a²Var(X) + b.
  • Adding variances of dependent variables without the 2·Cov term.
  • Using Binomial when there is no fixed number of trials — that is Poisson’s territory.
