    I

    The Normal Distribution

    The single most important probability distribution in statistics, engineering, AI and data science — also called the Gaussian or bell-shaped distribution. Master it first: every later topic (sampling, estimation, testing) stands on it.

    1.1Why do we need the Normal Distribution?

    Many real-world measurements tend to cluster around an average value: values near the average occur more frequently, while very small or very large values occur less frequently. Examples from the lectures:

    • Heights of students
    • Marks in a large examination
    • Manufacturing measurement errors
    • Sensor noise
    • Network response times (in some situations)
    • Measurement errors in IoT devices

    The exam-marks picture

    Suppose the average score is 70 out of 100. A large class then produces this pattern:

    Marks rangeNumber of students
    40–50Few
    50–60Some
    60–70Many
    70–80Many
    80–90Some
    90–100Few
     Few students at the extremes, many near the average — the signature “bump in the middle”.

    Tests such as the SAT and GRE famously produce this shape: the bulk of students score the average (C), creating the bump; smaller numbers score B or D, so the graph thins out moving away from the middle; an even smaller percentage score A or F, producing the thin tails. The result resembles a bell — hence the nickname. Salaries, measurement errors, heights and weights follow the same pattern, which is why the distribution is used so widely in business and science.

    1.2Definition, notation and the two parameters

    Definition

    A Normal Distribution is a continuous probability distribution in which most observations lie near the mean, and the probability gradually decreases as we move away from the mean. It is completely described by two parameters — the mean μ and the standard deviation σ.

    Notation — read “X is normally distributed with mean μ and variance σ²” X ~ N(μ, σ2)
    μ (mu)
    Mean (average) — the centre of the curve
    σ (sigma)
    Standard deviation — the spread of the curve
    σ2
    Variance (the square of the standard deviation)
    μ − σ
    One standard deviation below the mean
    μ + σ
    One standard deviation above the mean

    For example, if μ = 70 and σ = 10, the axis of the distribution reads like a ruler:

    40   50   60   70   80   90   100
    μ−3σ  μ−2σ  μ−σ  μ  μ+σ  μ+2σ  μ+3σ

    1.3The probability density function & key properties

    Probability density function (PDF) of the normal distribution f(x) = 1σ√2π e−(x − μ)² / (2σ²)

    You are rarely asked to evaluate this by hand — but you must know what it implies:

    Summary — properties of a normal distribution
    • Mean = median = mode. The highest point of the curve represents all three; they coincide.
    • Symmetry. The left side of the curve is an exact mirror image of the right side, about the centre μ.
    • Half and half. Exactly half of the values lie to the left of centre and exactly half to the right.
    • Total area = 1. The area under the whole curve is 1 (100%) — it represents all possible data.
    • Spread. A small σ makes the curve tall and narrow; a large σ makes it short and wide.

    Why P(X = exactly 70) = 0

    For a continuous distribution, the probability of any single exact value is zero:

    Continuous variables assign probability to intervals, not points P(X = 70) = 0   ·   ask instead: P(60 < X < 80)

    Why? Because there are infinitely many possible values — 69.999, 69.9999, 70, 70.0001, … — and the probability of 1 must be shared among infinitely many of them. Probability is therefore always calculated over an interval, as an area under the curve.

    Exam trap

    Never write P(X = x) > 0 for a normal (continuous) random variable. If a question asks for the probability of a single value, the answer is 0 — or the question really means a small interval around it.

    1.4The Empirical Rule: 68 – 95 – 99.7

    The empirical rule tells you what percentage of data falls within a certain number of standard deviations from the mean (valid only when the data are approximately normal):

    Within μ ± 1σ

    ≈ 68%
    (more precisely 68.27%)

    Within μ ± 2σ

    ≈ 95%
    (more precisely 95.45%)

    Within μ ± 3σ

    ≈ 99.7%
    (more precisely 99.73%)

    Worked exampleExam scores — X ~ N(70, 10²)

    Examination marks follow a normal distribution with mean 70 and standard deviation 10. Interpret the empirical rule, then apply it to 1,000 students.

    1. Within 1σ: 70 − 10 = 60 and 70 + 10 = 80, so 68% of students score between 60 and 80.
    2. Within 2σ: 70 − 2(10) = 50 and 70 + 2(10) = 90, so 95% of students score between 50 and 90.
    3. Scale to 1,000 students: approximately 680 students score between 60 and 80; approximately 950 score between 50 and 90.

    Note the pattern of the arithmetic: multiply σ by 1, 2, 3 — never by 10. A classic slip is writing 70 − 210 instead of 70 − 2(10).

    Exam trap

    The empirical rule is a property of normal distributions only. It does not apply to skewed data, and the “68–95–99.7” figures are approximations of 68.27–95.45–99.73.

    1.5μ sets the centre, σ sets the spread

    Consider two classes with the same average but different spreads: Class A has μ = 70, σ = 5; Class B has μ = 70, σ = 20. Both average 70 — but their distributions are completely different. The lesson (the slides call it the “PAS” picture):

    • Mean determines the centre — μ shifts the curve left or right.
    • Standard deviation determines the spread — σ changes the width.
    • Smaller σ → narrow and tall curve (data tightly clustered).
    • Larger σ → wide and flat curve (data spread out).

    The flat normal: “slab” vs “spike”

    In some experiments there is genuine uncertainty about where the centre of a distribution lies — for example in Bayesian analysis when prior information is scarce, or in any experiment with a dearth of data. Tornado researchers Elsner and Schroder used a flat normal distribution (and a flat Student’s-t) as priors in their 2019 studies, because tornado research was not then on a solid statistical foundation.

    Choosing a large variance produces a flat distribution — a “slab” — representing the researcher’s uncertainty about the parameter’s location. A distribution with a “spike” (small variance, sometimes called a peaked normal) represents strong prior confidence. Same machinery, opposite statements of belief.

    1.6The Standard Normal Distribution and Z-scores

    Definition

    The Standard Normal Distribution is the special normal distribution with μ = 0 and σ = 1. We write Z ~ N(0, 1).

    In the standard normal model, about 5% of data falls in the two tails (2.5% very low, 2.5% very high) and about 90% sits in between the ±1.96 boundaries.

    Why do we need Z at all?

    Different problems have different means and standard deviations — Dataset A might be N(70, 10²), Dataset B might be N(500, 50²). How can we compare a value from A with a value from B? We convert both to a common scale called the Z-score:

    The Z-score — how many standard deviations a value is from the mean Z = X − μσ   where X = actual value,  μ = mean,  σ = standard deviation
    • Z = 0 means the value sits exactly at the mean.
    • Z > 0 means the value is above the mean; Z < 0 means below.
    • |Z| tells you how unusual the value is — the heart of all anomaly detection.
    Worked exampleComputing Z-scores
    1. Student scores 85 (mean 70, σ = 10): Z = (85 − 70)/10 = 1.5 — the student scored 1.5 standard deviations above the mean.
    2. Student scores 50: Z = (50 − 70)/10 = −2 — 2 standard deviations below the mean. Negative Z = below average.
    3. Warm-up: if X = 1.2, mean = 0, σ = 1, then Z = (1.2 − 0)/1 = 1.2, and Φ(1.2) ≈ 88.5% (the area to the left).
    4. Server response time 260 ms (mean 200 ms, σ = 30 ms): Z = (260 − 200)/30 = 2 — 2 standard deviations slower than average; relatively unusual. The administrator may investigate server overload, network congestion, database delays or faulty microservices.

    How to compute a Z-score from raw data (4 steps)

    1. Find the mean μ — add all values and divide by the count.
    2. Find the standard deviation σ — how much the values typically vary from that mean.
    3. Subtract the mean — (X − μ) tells you the direction (positive = above, negative = below).
    4. Divide by σ — the result is the Z-score.

    Comparing scores across subjects — the classic application

    You score 90 in Math and 95 in English. You might think you are better in English. Not necessarily! Suppose Math has mean 80, σ = 5, while English has mean 90, σ = 5. Then:

    • Math: Z = (90 − 80)/5 = 2 → two standard deviations above the mean.
    • English: Z = (95 − 90)/5 = 1 → one standard deviation above the mean.

    In Math your score is far higher than most students (it falls into the tail) — you actually performed better in Math than in English. The standard deviation lets you compare distributions with different data and different means; only Z-scores, not raw marks, make scores comparable.

    1.7Applications: Z-scores as anomaly detectors

    The lecture builds three engineering scenarios around the same idea — compute a Z-score; if it is far from 0, investigate. They are prime exam material.

    ApplicationIoT sensor fault detection

    An IoT temperature sensor has normal readings μ = 25°C, σ = 2°C. The normal range within 3σ is 25 − 3(2) = 19°C to 25 + 3(2) = 31°C — this contains ≈ 99.7% of normal readings.

    1. The sensor reports 38°C. Compute Z: Z = (38 − 25)/2 = 6.5.
    2. Interpretation: a value 6.5 standard deviations from the mean is extremely unusual.
    3. Possible reasons: sensor malfunction, data transmission error, or an actual abnormal event.
    4. Where this is used: IoT anomaly detection, network monitoring, fraud detection, industrial monitoring.
    ApplicationCybersecurity anomaly detection

    Login attempts per minute for a user follow approximately μ = 5, σ = 2. The user suddenly makes 15 login attempts.

    1. Z = (15 − 5)/2 = 5 — far from normal behaviour.
    2. The cybersecurity system can flag this as potential suspicious activity.
    Note from the lecture (often asked as a viva question)

    Login attempts are discrete, not continuous — modelled better by the Poisson distribution or advanced anomaly-detection models. The normal model is used here to understand standardization and anomaly scoring.

    Worked examplePotato chip factory quality control

    Bags are packed with target μ = 400 g and σ = 10 g. An inspection bag weighs X = 425 g. Is this normal variation or an extreme outlier signalling machine malfunction?

    1. Compute: Z = (425 − 400)/10 = 25/10 = +2.5.
    2. Direction: positive → this bag is heavier than the factory average.
    3. Distance: exactly 2.5 standard deviations above the mean.
    4. Probability: roughly 99.38% of all bags weigh less than this; only about 0.62% of bags are this heavy by pure chance.
    5. Action plan: beyond ±2 is unusual and beyond ±3 is highly anomalous, so Z = +2.5 warns that the filling nozzle may be over-dispensing product — recalibrate the machine.
    Memory hook

    |Z| > 3 → investigate as a potential outlier. This simple rule powers the ML anomaly-detection pipeline: collect normal data → compute μ → compute σ → compute Z → flag large |Z|.

    1.8Φ(z) and reading the Z-table

    The symbol Φ(z) (capital phi) denotes the probability from −∞ up to z — the area to the left of z under the standard normal curve:

    The three probability formulas — know all three cold Left: P(Z < z) = Φ(z)   ·   Right: P(Z > z) = 1 − Φ(z)   ·   Between: P(a < Z < b) = Φ(b) − Φ(a)
    • Φ(0) = 0.5, because the standard normal is symmetric — 50% of the area lies left of the mean.
    • Φ(1.00) = P(Z ≤ 1.00) = 0.8413 from the table → 84.13% of the area lies left of Z = 1.

    Reading a cumulative Z-table

    A Z-table puts the probabilities (like that 50%) into standard-deviation units from the mean. To look up Z = 1.25: split it as 1.2 + 0.05 → go to row 1.2, column 0.05; the intersection gives 0.8944. So P(Z < 1.25) = 0.8944, i.e. 89.44% of the area lies to the left of Z = 1.25.

    Memory hook — row and column

    First digit(s) + first decimal → Row. Second decimal → Column. For Z = 1.25: row 1.2, column 0.05 → 0.8944.

    Exam trap — two different Z-tables exist

    Type A (cumulative left): directly gives P(Z < z). Example Z = 1.00 → table value 0.8413, so P(Z < 1) = 0.8413. Type B (mean-to-Z): gives the area between 0 and z. For Z = 1.00 it prints 0.3413, and you must add 0.5: P(Z < 1) = 0.5 + 0.3413 = 0.8413. Always check which Z-table you have been given.

    Worked exampleThree solved table look-ups
    1. Find P(X < 115) for X ~ N(100, 15²): standardise first — Z = (115 − 100)/15 = 1. Row 1.0, column 0.00 → Φ(1) = 0.8413. So P(X < 115) = 0.8413 (84.13%).
    2. Find P(Z > 1.25): total probability is 1, so P(Z > 1.25) = 1 − 0.8944 = 0.1056.
    3. Find P(0 < Z < 1.25): Φ(1.25) − Φ(0) = 0.8944 − 0.5 = 0.3944.

    Try these (from the lecture): find P(Z < 0.75), find P(Z > 1.50), and find P(−1 < Z < 1).

    Show solution
    • P(Z < 0.75) = Φ(0.75) = 0.7734
    • P(Z > 1.50) = 1 − Φ(1.50) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668
    • P(−1 < Z < 1) = Φ(1) − Φ(−1) = 0.8413 − 0.1587 = 0.6826 (the empirical 68%!) — or use symmetry: 2Φ(1) − 1.

    For negative z-values use symmetry: Φ(−z) = 1 − Φ(z). A full cumulative table from 0.0 to 3.4 is provided in Part VI.

    1.9The Normal Distribution in Machine Learning

    Normality assumptions appear all over ML — the lecture organises them into three families. This is a favourite theory question.

    1. Models assuming normality of errors / residuals

    • Linear Regression (OLS): assumes residual errors are normally distributed around zero with constant variance. Violation does not bias the coefficient estimates, but non-normal errors invalidate confidence intervals, hypothesis testing (p-values), and standard-error calculations.

    2. Models assuming normality of input features

    • Gaussian Naive Bayes: explicitly models continuous features with the Gaussian PDF; heavily skewed features make class-likelihood estimates highly inaccurate.
    • LDA & QDA: assume feature vectors within each class come from a multivariate normal; skewed or multimodal distributions make the decision boundaries perform poorly.

    3. Distance- and variance-based algorithms

    • PCA: seeks axes of maximum variance; severe skewness or outliers artificially inflate variance in one direction, so PCA relies on symmetric, Gaussian-like distributions to find meaningful components.
    • K-Means & KNN: rely on Euclidean distance; skewed distributions compress distances for most points while outliers dominate — suboptimal clusters and boundaries.
    • Logistic Regression & Neural Networks: optimizers like Gradient Descent converge faster and more reliably when features are normal (or standardized to N(0,1)).

    Gaussian noise

    Images and signals may contain Gaussian noise. The processing pipeline: Original image → add Gaussian noise → noisy image → denoising algorithm. Used in computer vision, image processing and signal processing.

    Collect normal data
    ↓
    Calculate mean (μ)
    ↓
    Calculate standard deviation (σ)
    ↓
    Calculate Z-score of new observations
    ↓
    Large |Z|?
    ↓
    Potential anomaly — investigate
    Key insight — algorithms that do NOT need normality

    Tree-based algorithms (Decision Trees, Random Forests, XGBoost, LightGBM) are non-parametric: they split data on feature thresholds (rank-order) rather than linear combinations or distances, making them completely immune to non-normal or skewed feature distributions.

    II

    Sampling & the Central Limit Theorem

    Syllabus: random sampling, sample mean, sample variance, the sampling distribution of the sample mean, standard error, and the CLT with intuitive examples. One sentence to hold onto: a good sample lets us learn about a large population without measuring everyone.

    2.1Why do we sample?

    Research question from the lectures: “How can we estimate the average response time of an entire cloud system?” The population may contain 1,00,000+ requests, and studying every request may be expensive, time-consuming, or sometimes impossible. Therefore:

    Population
    ↓
    Select sample
    ↓
    Analyse sample
    ↓
    Infer about population
    The soup analogy

    Entire pot = population · a spoonful = sample · the taste/measurement = data · the conclusion about the whole pot = statistical inference. You don’t drink the whole pot to know it needs salt.

    Scenario — average screen time of SY students: population = 1,200 SY students; sample = 100 selected students; variable = daily screen time. The population mean is the actual average of all 1,200; the sample mean is the average of the chosen 100. We use the sample to learn about the population.

    Census vs sampling

    Census

    Data collected from every member of the population; gives complete information; usually expensive and time-consuming.

    Sampling

    Data collected from only a subset; faster, cheaper, more practical — but introduces sampling uncertainty.

    All packets passing through a network1,000 selected packets
    All transactions in a banking system5,000 selected transactions
    All predictions made by an AI model500 randomly selected predictions

    2.3The sampling frame — and coverage bias

    Definition

    A sampling frame is the actual list or source from which we select the sample.

    Simple example: a university has 10,000 students and we survey 500. Population = all 10,000 students; sampling frame = the university student database containing the 10,000; sample = the 500 randomly selected from that database. Cybersecurity example: population = all network devices in the organization; frame = the registered device inventory / IP-address list; sample = 500 devices selected from the inventory.

    Memory hook

    Population = who we want to study · Frame = where we get the names/units to select from · Sample = who we actually select.

    A good sampling frame should be complete (includes all or nearly all population units), accurate (information is correct), and up-to-date (old units removed, new ones included).

    Exam trap — coverage bias

    If the frame excludes part of the population, coverage bias occurs. Example: population = all university students, but frame = only students who use the university mobile app. Students who never use the app have zero chance of selection — they are invisible to the study. Always ask: “Who is missing from our sampling frame?”

    2.4Random sampling and the golden rule

    Definition — Simple Random Sampling

    Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. Example: from 10,000 network requests, randomly select 500 using a random-number generator — with no preference for time, user, location, or request type.

    Why random sampling? It reduces systematic selection bias and helps make the sample more representative. Example: select 1,000 customers randomly from a database of 100,000.

    Key insight — the golden rule

    Never confuse “randomly collected” data with “randomly selected” data. “Randomly collected” is a loose phrase meaning data happened to arrive without an obvious pattern — it does not mean every member had a known, equal chance of selection. A company posting an online survey receives responses from customers who volunteer: the responses may look random, but the process is self-selected. Randomness must come from the selection mechanism — not from the appearance of the data.

    Why not just take the first 500?

    Morning traffic may differ from afternoon traffic, so the first 500 requests may not represent the whole day — this is sampling bias. Similarly, testing an AI model only on easy images produces unrealistically high accuracy. Always ask: “Does my sample actually represent the population I want to study?”

    2.5The five classical sampling designs

    1 · Simple Random Sampling (SRS)

    Advantages

    Simple concept · easy with digital databases · minimizes investigator selection bias.

    Limitations

    Requires a complete sampling frame · may not adequately represent small but important subgroups.

    Industry use: customer surveys, quality inspection, audit sampling, research studies.

    2 · Stratified Random Sampling

    Definition: divide the population into meaningful strata (homogeneous groups), then randomly sample within each stratum. A university that is 60% undergraduate, 25% postgraduate and 15% PhD uses stratified sampling to guarantee each group is represented.

    DomainPossible strataPurpose
    AI — facial recognitionAge group, gender, geographic region, lighting conditionModel not over-trained on one demographic; improves representation & fairness
    ML — fraud detectionFraudulent/legitimate, transaction type, region, customer segmentRare fraud patterns adequately represented
    IoT — smart home sensorsSensor type, device location, time of day, normal/abnormal operationCapture device diversity
    Data science — customer analyticsAge group, region, customer value, purchase frequencyHigh-value customers or one region don’t dominate
    Recommendation systemsNew vs existing users, heavy vs light users, category, regionRepresentative evaluation of algorithms
    Autonomous vehiclesDay/night, weather, road type, traffic density, urban/highwayDiverse real-world driving conditions
    ML — medical diagnosisDisease status, age group, gender, hospital/regionRepresentative diagnostic models
    IoT — predictive maintenanceMachine type, age, environment, healthy/faulty conditionLearn both normal and failure patterns
    Cybersecurity — intrusion detectionNormal traffic, DoS, malware, port scanning, phishingDataset isn’t 99.9% normal packets with almost no attacks
    Network security monitoringDepartment, device type, protocol, location, time periodCapture different network behaviours

    Why use stratified sampling? Improves representation of important subgroups; can reduce sampling variability; especially useful when groups differ substantially. Applications: market research, healthcare studies, government surveys, customer analytics.

    Memory hook

    “Divide first → randomly sample within groups.” Every stratum contributes; no group is skipped.

    3 · Systematic Sampling

    Definition: select observations at a fixed interval from an ordered population. Procedure: choose a random starting point, then select every k-th observation. Example: population = 10,000 customers, required sample = 1,000, so the sampling interval is k = 10,000 / 1,000 = 10 — select every 10th customer after a random start.

    • Advantages: simple, fast, easy to automate.
    • Risk: periodic patterns in the population can create bias (if every 10th customer happens to be a similar type, the sample is distorted).

    4 · Cluster Sampling

    Definition: divide the population into naturally occurring clusters, randomly select whole clusters, and study all members within the selected clusters. Instead of randomly selecting students across an entire country: select schools → survey all students in the selected schools. Why: reduces travel and operational cost; useful when members are geographically distributed. Applications: educational surveys, healthcare studies, geographic research, household surveys.

    Exam trap — stratified vs cluster

    Stratified: sample from EVERY stratum (groups are internally homogeneous, you pick a few members from each). Cluster: select SOME clusters entirely (groups are mini-populations, you pick a few groups and take them whole). If a researcher randomly selects observations from each traffic category — that is stratified. If a company randomly selects 5 branches and surveys all employees within them — that is cluster.

    5 · Multistage Sampling

    Definition: sampling performed through multiple stages, sampling within sampled units. International education survey example: Country → States → Districts → Schools → Students (few countries → 16 states → 40 districts → 200 schools → 250,000 students). Industry example: 9 states → 50 offices → 7 departments → 6,000 employees.

    • Advantages: efficient for very large populations; reduces logistical cost; suitable for geographically distributed populations.
    • Cluster vs multistage: multistage continues sampling through multiple levels (a sample of clusters, then a sample within each chosen cluster, and so on); plain cluster sampling stops after choosing the clusters.

    2.6Sampling bias and its friends

    Definition

    Sampling bias occurs when the method of selecting the sample systematically favours some members of the population. Examples: a network study selecting only high-speed networks; a cybersecurity study using only attacks recorded during office hours; an AI study testing only high-quality images; surveying only students who attend a particular lab.

    Result chain: biased sample → biased estimate → misleading research conclusion.

    Important point

    More data does not automatically remove bias. A million biased observations can still produce a biased conclusion. (This returns in big-data sampling: big ≠ unbiased.)

    Common causes of sampling bias: poor sampling frame · convenience sampling · self-selection · nonresponse · exclusion of certain groups.

    Selection bias

    Some members are systematically more likely to be included. E.g. an online survey about internet services: people with poor internet access are less likely to participate, so the sample over-represents highly connected users. In AI/ML: training data from a non-representative population produces biased models.

    Nonresponse bias

    Selected individuals don’t respond and respondents differ from non-respondents. E.g. a bank surveys customers about financial stress; customers in severe difficulty are less willing to respond, so observed responses underestimate true stress. Remedies: follow-up surveys, incentives, multiple channels, nonresponse weighting.

    Coverage bias

    The frame doesn’t cover the target population. E.g. frame = only households with registered landline numbers; some households have zero probability of selection. Key question: “Who is missing from our sampling frame?”

    2.7Sampling error and sample size

    Definition

    Sampling error is the natural difference between a sample statistic and the corresponding population parameter, caused by observing only a sample. Example: true population mean μ = ₹50,000 but sample mean x̄ = ₹49,200 — the difference is sampling error.

    Important

    Sampling error is not necessarily a mistake — it is an inherent consequence of sampling. Larger, well-designed samples generally reduce sampling variability.

    Sample size determination — “How much data do we need?”

    Too small

    High uncertainty · low statistical power · unstable estimates.

    Too large

    Higher cost · more time · potentially unnecessary data collection.

    Sample size depends on: desired precision · confidence level · population variability · effect size · statistical power · population size · cost of data collection.

    Industry principle

    Sample size should be designed — not guessed.

    2.8Sample mean and sampling variability

    The sample mean x = Σ xin   where xi = individual observation, n = sample size
    Worked exampleResponse times of 5 requests

    100, 120, 130, 150, 200 ms

    x = (100 + 120 + 130 + 150 + 200) / 5 = 700 / 5 = 140 ms — “the average response time of these 5 samples is 140 ms.”

    140 ms is NOT necessarily the true population mean — it is an estimate. Applications: average sales, temperature, response time, manufacturing measurement, customer spending.

    The tricky research question — sample variability

    Four samples give means of 140, 146, 137 and 143 ms. Which is the “correct” sample mean? All of them — each is correct for its own sample. They differ because the samples contain different observations. This is sampling variability: different samples → different statistics. It is the foundation of everything that follows.

    2.9Sample variance — and why n − 1

    The sample variance — spread of observations around the sample mean s2 = Σ (xi − x)2n − 1

    Five steps: (1) calculate the sample mean; (2) find each deviation from the mean; (3) square each deviation; (4) add the squared deviations; (5) divide by n − 1.

    Worked exampleSample variance of 10, 12, 14 ms
    1. Mean: x = (10 + 12 + 14)/3 = 12 ms.
    2. Deviations: 10 − 12 = −2; 12 − 12 = 0; 14 − 12 = +2.
    3. Squared deviations: 4, 0, 4 → sum = 8.
    4. Sample variance: s2 = 8 / (3 − 1) = 4 ms².

    Why divide by n − 1 and not n?

    Because the sample mean is itself estimated from the same sample. Once x is calculated, the deviations from it are not completely free to vary — only n − 1 deviations are effectively independent. This is called degrees of freedom. Using n − 1 gives an unbiased estimator of the population variance under the usual random-sampling assumptions: dividing by n would systematically underestimate the population variance.

    Memory hook

    Population variance → divide by N. Sample variance → divide by n − 1. Estimating the sample mean “uses up” one degree of freedom.

    Three observations have a fixed sample mean. Once two deviations from the mean are known, what happens to the third deviation?

    Show solution

    It is determined by the other two — because the deviations must sum to zero. Hence only n − 1 = 2 deviations are free to vary.

    Unbiased estimation

    Definition

    An estimator is unbiased if its expected value equals the true population parameter. Intuition: repeatedly take samples and calculate the estimate; if the average of those estimates equals the true parameter, the estimator is unbiased. The usual sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean.

    Exam trap

    Unbiased does not mean accurate for every individual sample. An unbiased estimator can still have considerable variability — unbiasedness is about the long-run average, not about any single estimate.

    Interpretation of variance: small variance → observations closely grouped; large variance → widely dispersed. Mean tells us the centre; variance tells us the spread. Applications: financial risk, manufacturing consistency, sensor variability, measurement uncertainty, model evaluation.

    2.10The big idea: repeated sampling and the sampling distribution

    Imagine a population of millions of network requests. Take a random sample of 100 requests and calculate x₁. Put the sample back. Take another random sample of 100 → x₂. Repeat: x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄, … Now we have MANY sample means. What happens if we study the distribution of these sample means?

    Definition

    A sampling distribution describes how a statistic behaves across repeated samples from the same population. Take a sample → calculate a statistic (e.g. the mean) → repeat hundreds or thousands of times. The collection of those statistics forms the sampling distribution.

    Key insight

    We are no longer studying individual observations — we are studying the behaviour of a statistic.

    Population distributionSampling distribution
    ContainsIndividual observations (100, 120, 130, 150, 200, …)Sample means (140, 146, 137, 143, 141, …)
    Shows variation amongIndividual observationsSample means

    Sampling distribution of the sample mean

    Definition

    The probability distribution of x obtained from all possible random samples of a fixed size n from a population. Example: population = response times of all requests; repeated samples of 100 requests give 240, 245, 238, 242, 241, 239, … — these values form the sampling distribution of x.

    Important properties (under standard conditions):

    • Centre: centred at the population mean μ.
    • Spread: equals the standard error.
    • Shape: becomes approximately normal as sample size increases (this is the CLT).

    Why it matters: in research we usually have only ONE sample, but we want to know “how reliable is my sample mean?” The sampling distribution answers it — how much sample means vary, how close x tends to be to μ, how much uncertainty exists. This leads directly to the standard error, confidence intervals and hypothesis testing.

    The sample variance also has a sampling distribution

    Different samples produce different estimates of variability (sample 1 → s₁², sample 2 → s₂², …). A factory measuring 5 bolts at a time gets a different variance each time: 10.1, 10.3, 10.0, 10.2, 10.4 mm then 10.2, 10.1, 10.5, 10.3, 10.4 mm, and so on. So never think “the sample variance IS the population variance” — it is an estimate with uncertainty. For normally distributed populations, the sample variance is closely connected to the chi-square distribution (advanced connection).

    Caution exampleMachine A vs Machine B variability

    Machine A has estimated variance s² = 0.04; Machine B has s² = 0.06. Can we immediately say Machine B is definitely more variable?

    No. The difference might be caused by sampling variation. We need to understand the uncertainty around the variance estimates — this is where sampling distributions, confidence intervals and statistical inference become important. (IoT parallel: is high vibration variance a real machine problem, or just limited observations?)

    2.11Standard error (SE)

    Definition

    Standard error measures the sampling variability of a statistic. Think of it as: “How much would my estimate change if I repeated the sampling process?” Smaller SE → more precise estimate; larger SE → less precise estimate.

    Standard error of the sample mean (σ known) SE(x) = σ√n

    Interpretation: SE tells us how much the sample mean typically varies across repeated samples of size n.

    Standard deviation (SD)Standard error (SE)
    MeasuresVariability of individual observationsVariability of a statistic (e.g. the sample mean)
    Question it answers“How spread out are the individual data values?”“How much would my sample mean vary if I repeatedly took samples?”
    MemoriseSD → spread of dataSE → spread of sample means

    Effect of sample size — and the “4× rule”

    Worked exampleHalving the standard error
    1. Suppose σ = 20 ms and n = 100: SE = 20/√100 = 2 ms.
    2. Increase to n = 400: SE = 20/√400 = 1 ms.
    3. Conclusion: sample size ×4 → standard error ×½.
    The “4 times sample size” rule

    Because SE ∝ 1/√n, cutting the SE in half requires about four times the sample size. To get SE = 1 ms from SE = 2 ms you need 400, not 200, observations.

    Research exampleAverage response time of a cloud application

    Population: all requests received during one month. Sample: 400 randomly selected requests. Sample mean x̄ = 250 ms, sample standard deviation s = 40 ms.

    SE ≈ s/√n = 40/√400 = 40/20 = 2 ms. Interpretation: 250 ms estimates the population mean, with sampling variability of about 2 ms. The same logic estimates model accuracy from sampled test data (ML), attack rate from sampled traffic (cybersecurity), average sensor temperature (IoT), average response time (cloud), average defect rate (software engineering), average packet delay (networks).

    Standard error of the sample proportion

    Standard error of the proportion p̂ SE(p̂) ≈ √ p̂(1 − p̂)n
    Worked exampleProduct A preference survey

    Survey 400 customers: 240 prefer Product A, 160 prefer Product B.

    1. Sample proportion: p̂ = 240/400 = 0.60 (60%).
    2. Repeat the survey with 400 different customers each time and you might get 60%, 57%, 63%, 59%, 61% … the proportion changes because the samples differ — SE measures this sample-to-sample variation.
    3. SE(p̂) ≈ √(0.60 × 0.40 / 400) = √0.0006 ≈ 0.0245 = 2.45%.
    4. Meaning: the sample proportion typically varies by about 2.45 percentage points from sample to sample due to sampling variation.

    Applications: election polling, customer preference, defect rates, conversion rates, A/B testing.

    Worked exampleAverage customer spending

    Population standard deviation σ = ₹1,000, sample size n = 100.

    SE(x̄) = 1000/√100 = ₹100. If we repeatedly take random samples of 100 customers, the sample means will typically vary around the population mean with a standard deviation of about ₹100. And the CLT tells us the distribution of these sample means can be approximately normal under suitable conditions.

    Finite population correction (FPC)

    The SE formulas assume the sample is a relatively small fraction of the population. When the sample is a large fraction, observations are no longer effectively independent in the same way, and we apply:

    Finite population correction SEcorrected = σ√n × √ N − nN − 1

    Intuition: if you sample almost everyone, there is very little uncertainty left. Sampling 900 people from a population of 1,000 gives far more information than sampling 900 from 1 million (where the correction is negligible).

    2.12The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

    Opening question from the lecture: “If the population is NOT normally distributed, can we still use the Normal distribution?” — Yes / No / Sometimes?

    Answer

    Sometimes — and the Central Limit Theorem explains exactly when and why.

    Central Limit Theorem — statement

    When the sample size becomes sufficiently large, the sampling distribution of the sample mean tends to become approximately normal under common conditions, even if the original population is not normal.

    The most important sentence

    CLT is about the distribution of SAMPLE MEANS — not the distribution of the original data. Original data does not need to be normal; with sufficiently large random samples, the distribution of x̄ → approximately Normal. This is one of the most important ideas behind statistical inference.

    Any reasonable population (skewed, irregular, non-normal)
    ↓  repeated sampling
    Sample means
    ↓  larger sample size
    Approximately Normal distribution

    Intuition — daily screen time

    Individual screen time is highly skewed: most people use 2–5 hours, some use 10–15 hours.

    • 1 person: screen time is unpredictable.
    • 2 people: average = (2 + 10)/2 = 6 hours — extreme values already have less influence.
    • 10 people: the average becomes more stable; one extreme value has a smaller effect.
    • 100 people: the sample mean is very stable. Repeat with many samples of 100 and the distribution of these means becomes bell-shaped.
    CLT intuition in one line

    “Averages behave more regularly than individual observations.” Individual data can be irregular or skewed, but averages of sufficiently large random samples tend to have a stable, approximately Normal distribution.

    The three distributions (make sure you can draw this)

    1. Distribution 1 — population: the actual individual observations (e.g. customer spending); can be highly skewed, irregular, non-normal.
    2. Distribution 2 — one sample: 100 individual spending values; this sample may still look skewed.
    3. Distribution 3 — sampling distribution: repeatedly take samples of 100, calculate each mean, and the distribution of Mean₁, Mean₂, Mean₃, … is the sampling distribution of the sample mean — what CLT talks about.

    CLT with real-world examples

    • Customer spending: individual purchases highly skewed, but averages of sufficiently large random samples tend toward normal.
    • Manufacturing: individual component measurements fluctuate; average measurements from repeated samples become predictable.
    • Healthcare: individual patient measurements vary substantially; sample averages exhibit approximately normal behaviour under suitable conditions.

    When to be careful — CLT works under suitable conditions

    Caution 1 — very small samples

    Especially with highly skewed populations, tiny samples don’t average the skewness away.

    Caution 2 — extreme outliers

    A few extreme observations can strongly influence the mean.

    Caution 3 — dependent observations

    Time-series observations may be correlated; CLT assumes independence.

    Caution 4 — non-representative sampling

    A biased sample does not become unbiased simply because it is large. CLT reduces sampling variability; it does not fix sampling bias.

    Exam trap — all of these statements are FALSE
    • “CLT makes the population normal.” (No — only the sampling distribution of the mean.)
    • “n = 30 always guarantees CLT.” (No magic number — it depends on the population shape.)
    • “CLT removes bias.” (No — it reduces variability, not bias.)
    • “More observations always mean perfect results.”
    • “CLT applies only to normally distributed populations.” (No — that is the whole point: it works for non-normal populations.)

    CLT does not say the population becomes normal

    It says the sampling distribution of the sample mean can become approximately normal. A strongly right-skewed population stays right-skewed; but take repeated samples, calculate the means, and the distribution of those means becomes increasingly symmetric as conditions improve. (Very heavy-tailed distributions, dependence, or small samples can require much larger n or alternative methods.)

    CLT + Standard Error — the perfect partnership

    CLT tells us

    What shape the sampling distribution has: x̄ ≈ Normal(μ, SE(x̄)).

    SE tells us

    How spread out that sampling distribution is.

    CLT → Confidence intervals — the bridge

    Worked exampleFrom CLT to a confidence interval

    Sample mean = ₹5,000, SE = ₹100. Under suitable conditions, CLT lets us use a normal approximation for the sampling distribution, leading to: estimate ± margin of error.

    1. For a 95% normal-based interval: x ± 1.96 × SE.
    2. ₹5,000 ± 1.96 × ₹100 ≈ ₹5,000 ± ₹196.
    3. Interval: approximately ₹4,804 to ₹5,196.

    This is why CLT is so important — it provides the foundation for confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, quality control, polling, business analytics and A/B testing. Practical question to ask: “Is my sample large enough for the normal approximation to be reasonable?” The answer depends on the population distribution, dependence structure, and statistic — not a universal cut-off.

    2.13Beyond classical sampling — the modern toolkit

    Bootstrap sampling

    Definition

    Bootstrap sampling is a resampling technique that repeatedly samples from the observed dataset with replacement. Original sample A B C D E → bootstrap samples B B D A E, then C A A D E, and so on — repeated many times.

    Why? To understand the variability of an estimator without relying entirely on theoretical formulas. For each bootstrap sample calculate the mean → Mean₁, Mean₂, Mean₃, … → plot them → the bootstrap distribution. Uses: confidence intervals, standard errors, uncertainty estimation, complex statistics.

    The resampling family

    Resampling methods repeatedly create new datasets from observed or simulated data: Bootstrap · Permutation tests · Jackknife · Cross-validation · Monte Carlo methods. They estimate uncertainty, sampling distributions, model performance and statistical significance — especially valuable when analytical formulas are difficult or unavailable.

    Monte Carlo sampling

    Monte Carlo methods use repeated random sampling and simulation to estimate probabilities, distributions or numerical quantities: randomly simulate → calculate → repeat → aggregate. Example: simulate 1 million possible system outcomes and estimate the probability of system failure. Applications: risk analysis, finance, engineering, reliability, operations research, AI and simulation.

    Sampling for experimental vs observational studies

    Experimental studies

    Select participants so treatment effects can be estimated reliably: eligible population → random sample → random assignment → treatment / control. Key concepts: random sampling, random assignment, control groups, replication, blinding, statistical power. Random sampling → generalizability; random assignment → causal inference.

    Observational studies

    Researchers observe existing conditions without assigning treatments (smoking & health, customer behaviour, productivity, economic data). Main challenge: association does not necessarily imply causation. Issues: confounding, selection bias, measurement bias, missing data.

    Sampling in big data — and in streams

    “If we have millions of records, why sample?” Because even large datasets can be expensive to process, redundant, biased, difficult to label, and computationally demanding. Applications: exploratory analysis, model prototyping, data quality checks, visualization, human annotation. Key insight: big data ≠ unbiased data.

    Streaming data: IoT sensors, website clicks, financial transactions, social media events and network traffic continuously generate data. Sampling approaches: reservoir sampling, time-based sampling, random sampling, stratified sampling, window-based sampling. Goal: maintain a useful representation of continuously changing data without storing everything.

    Rare events, importance sampling and imbalanced data

    • Rare events: fraud, equipment failures, cyber attacks, rare diseases, defective products. If only 0.1% of transactions are fraud, a random sample may contain almost no fraud cases — use specialized strategies to ensure representation.
    • Importance sampling: deliberately sample more frequently from important or rare regions, then apply appropriate weights: sample the important region more often → correct using weights. Applications: rare-event simulation, Bayesian computation, reliability engineering, reinforcement learning, Monte Carlo estimation. Key idea: sample strategically, then compensate mathematically.
    • Imbalanced data: 99% normal vs 1% fraudulent transactions — a model predicting “normal” every time achieves 99% accuracy but is practically useless. Techniques: oversampling, undersampling, stratified sampling, synthetic sampling, class-weighted approaches. Key lesson: accuracy can be misleading when classes are highly imbalanced.

    Train–validation–test and cross-validation

    SplitPurposeCritical rule
    Training setLearn model parametersThe test set must remain unseen until final evaluation. Common problem: data leakage — information from the test set accidentally influences model development.
    Validation setTune / select the model
    Test setFinal unbiased performance evaluation

    K-fold cross-validation: divide data into K folds → train on K − 1 folds → validate on the remaining fold → repeat K times → average the performance. Advantages: better use of limited data, more stable performance estimate, useful for model selection and hyperparameter tuning. Applications: ML, predictive analytics, feature selection, model comparison.

    III

    Point Estimation & Confidence Intervals

    A point estimate is a best guess; a confidence interval is a best guess with an admission of uncertainty. Confidence intervals quantify the uncertainty around point estimates and are widely used in research, business forecasting and quality control.

    3.1Point estimates

    Definition

    A point estimate is a single value used to approximate an unknown population parameter. It is typically the value obtained from a sample that serves as the best guess for the corresponding population parameter — e.g. the sample mean used to estimate the population mean.

    Worked exampleAverage height of university students

    We want the average height of all students, but cannot measure everyone.

    1. Take a random sample of 30 students and calculate the sample mean height.
    2. If the sample mean is 168 cm, that single number is the point estimate of the population mean height.
    Limitation — the reason CIs exist

    A point estimate is easy to calculate, but it provides no information about the possible error or uncertainty in the estimate. 168 cm could be off by 1 cm or by 10 cm — the point estimate alone cannot say. For that, we need confidence intervals.

    3.2Confidence intervals — concept and vocabulary

    Definition

    A confidence interval (CI) is a range of values likely to contain the true population parameter with a certain level of confidence. It gives a measure of uncertainty around the point estimate, is constructed from sample data, and is expressed as a range around that estimate.

    • Lower bound: the smallest value that could be the population parameter.
    • Upper bound: the largest value that could be the population parameter.
    • Level of confidence (typically 95% or 99%): how confident we are that the interval contains the true parameter.

    Z-critical vs T-critical — choosing the multiplier

    Use the T-critical value when

    The sample size is small (n < 30) or the population standard deviation σ is unknown.

    Use the Z-score when

    σ is known (or the sample is large enough to treat s as σ). The Z-score determines how many standard deviations from the mean we must go to achieve the desired confidence level.

    Confidence interval for a population mean μ x ± zα/2 × σ√n    (σ known / large n)  ·  x ± tα/2, n−1 × s√n    (σ unknown / small n)

    The critical values to memorise

    99%2.5760.005

    Interpreting a CI — the exam answer template

    Interpretation exampleAverage weight — 72 kg

    A sample of n = 25 students has mean weight x̄ = 72 kg with σ = 4.9 kg. The 95% CI is 72 ± 1.96 × (4.9/√25) = 72 ± 1.96 × 0.98 = 72 ± 1.92.

    • Point estimate: the sample mean (72 kg) is our best guess of the true average weight.
    • Confidence interval: the interval from 70.08 kg to 73.92 kg gives a range within which we are 95% confident the true population mean lies.
    Exam trap — the most common CI misstatement

    Do not say “there is a 95% probability that μ lies in this interval” in the naive sense — μ is a fixed number, not a random variable. The correct statement: “We are 95% confident that the interval contains the true mean; if we repeated the sampling procedure many times, about 95% of such intervals would contain μ.”

    3.3Worked examples — Z-based intervals (σ known)

    Worked example 1Mathematics test — 95% CI

    A random sample of 50 students from a large population has sample mean score 75 on a mathematics test; the population standard deviation is 10. Construct a 95% CI for the population mean.

    1. SE = σ/√n = 10/√50 = 10/7.071 = 1.414.
    2. Margin = 1.96 × 1.414 = 2.77.
    3. CI = 75 ± 2.77 → (72.23, 77.77)
    Worked example 2Bolts — 99% CI

    A factory produces bolts; a random sample of 100 bolts has average length 5 cm. The population standard deviation is 0.5 cm. Construct a 99% CI for the true mean length.

    1. SE = 0.5/√100 = 0.5/10 = 0.05.
    2. Margin = 2.576 × 0.05 = 0.129.
    3. CI = 5 ± 0.129 → (4.871, 5.129)

    Notice: higher confidence (99% vs 95%) → larger critical value → wider interval. Precision is the price of confidence.

    Worked example 3Apples — 90% CI

    The average weight of 40 apples in a supermarket is 150 grams; the population standard deviation is known to be 20 grams. Find the 90% CI for the population mean weight.

    1. SE = 20/√40 = 20/6.325 = 3.162.
    2. Margin = 1.645 × 3.162 = 5.20.
    3. CI = 150 ± 5.20 → (144.8, 155.2)
    Worked example 4Batteries — 98% CI

    A sample of 64 batteries has mean lifespan 500 hours; the population standard deviation is 30 hours. Find the 98% CI.

    1. SE = 30/√64 = 30/8 = 3.75.
    2. Margin = 2.33 × 3.75 = 8.74.
    3. CI = 500 ± 8.74 → (491.26, 508.74)
    Worked example 5Cholesterol — 95% CI

    A medical study finds the average cholesterol level of a sample of 81 individuals is 190 mg/dL; the population standard deviation is 36 mg/dL. Determine the 95% CI.

    1. SE = 36/√81 = 36/9 = 4.
    2. Margin = 1.96 × 4 = 7.84.
    3. CI = 190 ± 7.84 → (182.16, 197.84)

    3.4Worked examples — s used, and the t-based interval

    Worked example 6Student heights — 95% CI with s

    A researcher wants the average height of students. A random sample of 50 students has mean height 168 cm with standard deviation 8 cm. Compute the 95% CI for the population mean.

    1. n = 50 is large, so we may use Z with s in place of σ: SE = 8/√50 = 1.131.
    2. Margin = 1.96 × 1.131 = 2.22.
    3. CI = 168 ± 2.22 → (165.78, 170.22)
    Worked example 7Test scores, n = 15 — the t-based interval

    A sample of 15 students has an average test score of 78 with a standard deviation of 10. Construct a 95% CI for the population mean.

    1. n = 15 < 30 and σ is unknown → use the T-critical value with df = n − 1 = 14: t0.025, 14 ≈ 2.145.
    2. SE = s/√n = 10/√15 = 2.582.
    3. Margin = 2.145 × 2.582 = 5.54.
    4. CI = 78 ± 5.54 → (72.46, 83.54)

    If your course uses the z shortcut here (1.96 × 2.582 = 5.06 → (72.94, 83.06)), state the assumption — but the t-interval is the statistically correct choice for small n with unknown σ, exactly as the lecture’s rule specifies.

    P2. A machine produces components with a standard deviation of 1.6 cm in length. A random sample of 64 components has a mean length of 90 cm. The customer rejects parts below 88 cm or above 92 cm. Does the 95% CI for the true mean length ensure acceptance by the customer?

    Show solution

    Here μ is the mean length of components in the population. SE = 1.6/√64 = 0.2, so:

    90 − (1.96 × 0.2) ≤ μ ≤ 90 + (1.96 × 0.2) → 90 ± 0.392

    89.61 ≤ μ ≤ 90.39

    The probability that the true population mean length falls in (89.61, 90.39) at 95% confidence — and since the entire interval lies comfortably inside the customer’s acceptance band (88, 92), the 95% CI ensures acceptance of the components by the customer.

    P3. A sample of 100 measurements of the breaking strength of cotton thread gave a mean of 7.4 and a standard deviation of 1.2 gms. Find the 95% confidence limits for the mean breaking strength.

    Show solution

    SE = 1.2/√100 = 0.12; margin = 1.96 × 0.12 = 0.2352.

    7.4 ± 0.2352 → (7.165, 7.635)

    The true value of the population mean breaking strength falls in this interval at 95% confidence.

    P4. The mean lifetime of a sample of 169 light bulbs is 1350 hours with a standard deviation of 100 hours. Establish 90% confidence limits within which the mean lifetime is expected to lie.

    Show solution

    Given n = 169, x̄ = 1350, s = 100. Significance level = (100 − 90)% = 10%, so α = 0.1 and the significant value is zα/2 = 1.645.

    SE = 100/√169 = 100/13 = 7.692; margin = 1.645 × 7.692 = 12.65.

    1350 ± 12.65 → (1337.35, 1362.65)

    The mean lifetime of the bulbs is expected to lie between 1337.35 and 1362.65 hours.

    P5 (drill). Verify the pattern: for a fixed confidence level, what happens to the width of the CI when n is quadrupled?

    Show solution

    Width = 2 × critical × σ/√n ∝ 1/√n. Quadrupling n halves the SE and therefore halves the width — the “4× sample size” rule from Part II. To halve the margin of error at the same confidence, you need four times the data.

    Tests Involving the Normal Distribution

    The key engineering question: is an observed difference due to random variation, or is there a real change in the system? Hypothesis testing is the formal machinery for answering it — with H₀, H₁, α, one- and two-tailed Z-tests, and P-values.

    4.1The problem: can we trust the sample?

    Scenario — engineer monitoring a cloud service. The company claims the average API response time is 200 ms. You collect data and find the average of sampled requests is 215 ms. Is this 15 ms difference just random variation, or has system performance actually changed? This is exactly what hypothesis testing helps decide.

    Normal random variation → no strong evidence of a real change.

    Situation B — large difference

    Definition

    A hypothesis is a testable statement or claim about a population parameter (μ = 200, p = 0.95, μ = 500). Hypothesis testing is a statistical procedure that uses sample data to evaluate such a claim — a method of deciding whether an observed difference is likely due to random variation or provides sufficient evidence of a real change in the population.

    4.2H₀ and H₁ — the two hypotheses

    Null hypothesis H₀

    Represents the current assumption, claim, or normal condition — “nothing unusual has happened”. Server example: company claims μ = 200 ms → H₀: μ = 200. Quality example: machine normally produces μ = 100 mm → H₀: μ = 100, the process is operating normally.

    Alternative hypothesis H₁

    The possibility that we want to investigate — “something has changed”. It comes in three flavours (≠, >, <) which determine the type of test.

    Memory hook

    H₀ = status quo (the claim on trial). H₁ = the change you suspect. The entire test is a trial of H₀ with the sample as evidence.

    4.3One-tailed vs two-tailed tests

    The single question that decides everything: in which direction are we looking for a change?

    Two-tailed test

    H₁: μ ≠ μ₀ — changed in either direction (faster or slower, higher or lower). Critical values ±1.96 at α = 0.05.

    Right-tailed test

    H₁: μ > μ₀ — looking specifically for an increase (latency up, failures up, life up). Critical value +1.645.

    Left-tailed test

    H₁: μ < μ₀ — looking specifically for a decrease (execution time down, defects down, energy down). Critical value −1.645.

    Worked identification examples

    • Performance changed? API response time may differ in either direction: H₀: μ = 200 vs H₁: μ ≠ 200 → two-tailed.
    • IoT sensor calibration: a temperature sensor should average 25°C; calibration may have drifted high or low → H₁: μ ≠ 25 → two-tailed. Key applications: sensor calibration, manufacturing process monitoring, software performance regression testing, network performance changes.
    • Response time increased? H₀: μ = 200 vs H₁: μ > 200 → right-tailed.
    • New compiler / algorithm reduces execution time? old mean 10 seconds: H₀: μ = 10 vs H₁: μ < 10 → left-tailed. Applications: performance improvement testing, defect reduction, energy consumption reduction, latency reduction.
    How to choose — three questions

    Do I care only about an increase? → right-tailed. Only about a decrease? → left-tailed. About any change? → two-tailed.

    4.4Significance level α and the two error types

    Definition — level of significance

    The significance level α tells us how much risk we are willing to accept when rejecting H₀. Common value: α = 0.05 (5%). Meaning: we reject H₀ only if the observed result is sufficiently unusual — the threshold is set so that, under H₀, the probability of incorrectly rejecting it is controlled at 5%.

    Fraud-detection analogy: if an automated flagging threshold is too sensitive, many normal transactions are incorrectly flagged (false alarms); if too relaxed, real fraud goes undetected. α controls the willingness to make a false alarm.

    We do not say “Accept H₀”. We say “Fail to reject H₀.” Insufficient evidence does not prove H₀ is true — just as a “not guilty” verdict does not prove innocence. Similarly the safe region is the failure-to-reject region, not the “acceptance region”.

    4.5The Z-test statistic

    Definition

    A test statistic converts the sample evidence into a numerical score, answering: how far is our sample result from what H₀ claims? For a population mean (σ known, or appropriate large-sample conditions), we use the Z-test.

    Z-test statistic for a population mean Z = x − μ0σ / √n   where x = sample mean · μ₀ = claimed mean · σ = population SD · n = sample size

    The denominator σ/√n is the standard error (Part II). So the test statistic measures: how many standard errors the sample mean is away from the claimed mean.

    Small |Z|

    Sample result close to the claimed value → likely normal variation.

    Large |Z|

    Sample result far from the claimed value → possibly significant difference.

    Worked exampleServer performance — computing Z

    A cloud service claims μ = 200 ms with known population standard deviation σ = 40 ms. A sample of n = 64 requests has x̄ = 215 ms. Calculate the test statistic.

    1. SE = σ/√n = 40/√64 = 40/8 = 5.
    2. Z = (215 − 200)/5 = 15/5 = 3.
    3. Meaning of Z = 3: the sample mean is 3 standard errors above the claimed mean — quite far from the centre under H₀. But how far is far enough? For that we need a critical value.

    4.6Critical values and rejection regions

    Definition

    A critical value is the boundary between normal/expected variation and statistically unusual variation. The rejection region contains test-statistic values considered too extreme under H₀; the region between the critical values is the failure-to-reject region.

    For a two-tailed test with α = 0.05, the critical values are approximately −1.96 and +1.96. The rejection regions are Z < −1.96 or Z > 1.96; the failure-to-reject region is −1.96 ≤ Z ≤ 1.96.

    Why is the two-tailed boundary further from centre? With α = 0.05 in one tail, the boundary is 1.645. In a two-tailed test the 5% is split into two tails of 0.025 each — so each boundary must be further from the centre: ±1.96.

    Two-tailed (H₁: μ ≠ μ₀)±1.96Z < −1.96 or Z > 1.96

    3. Interpretation: there is sufficient statistical evidence that the average API response time differs from the claimed 200 ms. Since x̄ = 215 ms is higher, the company should investigate: increased traffic, database bottleneck, network congestion, software regression, infrastructure problems.
    Industrial framing

    Hypothesis testing as a monitoring system: assume the process is normal (H₀) → collect sample data → if the result is too unusual to reasonably attribute to random variation, reject H₀ and investigate the process.

    4.7The 8-step recipe (with the battery example)

    Master recipeTesting a claim about a population mean

    A company claims the average battery lifetime is 10 hours, with known σ = 2 hours. Suppose n = 100 and x̄ = 9.6 hours; check whether the lifetime has changed at α = 0.05.

    1. State the industry problem: claimed μ₀ = 10 hours, σ = 2 known.
    2. Formulate hypotheses (change in either direction): H₀: μ = 10 vs H₁: μ ≠ 10 → two-tailed test.
    3. Choose the significance level: α = 0.05.
    4. Collect sample data: n = 100, x̄ = 9.6.
    5. Calculate the Z-statistic: SE = 2/√100 = 0.2; Z = (9.6 − 10)/0.2 = −0.4/0.2 = −2.
    6. Find critical values: for two-tailed α = 0.05 → Zcritical = ±1.96.
    7. Statistical decision: −2 < −1.96 → falls in the left rejection region → Reject H₀.
    8. Industry decision: sufficient evidence that the average battery lifetime differs from 10 hours; since x̄ = 9.6 < 10, the evidence suggests it may have decreased.

    4.8P-values — the second decision method

    Definition

    The P-value tells us: how compatible is the observed data with the null hypothesis? A small P-value means the observed result would be relatively unusual if H₀ were true.

    Decision rule (commonly α = 0.05) p < α ⇒ Reject H₀   ·   p ≥ α ⇒ Not enough evidence to reject H₀

    P-value depends on the test type — memorise this table

    TestHypothesesP-value formula
    Right-tailedH₁: μ > μ₀p = P(Z ≥ Zobserved) = 1 − Φ(Z)
    Left-tailedH₁: μ < μ₀p = P(Z ≤ Zobserved) = Φ(Z)
    Two-tailedH₁: μ ≠ μ₀p = 2 × P(Z ≥ |Zobserved|) = 2[1 − Φ(|Z|)]

    Critical-value method vs P-value method

    Method 1 — critical value

    Calculate test statistic → compare with critical value → reject / fail to reject H₀.

    Method 2 — P-value

    Calculate test statistic → find the P-value → compare with α → decision.

    Key insight

    Both methods should lead to the same decision when applied consistently: Z beyond the critical value ⟺ p < α. The P-value additionally tells you how strong the evidence is.

    4.9Six industry case studies — both methods, fully worked

    Case study 1Cloud server latency — right-tailed

    A cloud company claims μ = 200 ms. The engineering team suspects response time has increased (worse). Given σ = 40, n = 64, x̄ = 212 ms, α = 0.05.

    1. Hypotheses: H₀: μ = 200 vs H₁: μ > 200 (only an increase matters) → right-tailed Z-test.
    2. Statistic: SE = 40/√64 = 5; Z = (212 − 200)/5 = 12/5 = 2.4.
    3. Critical-value method: right-tailed critical value = 1.645. Since 2.4 > 1.645 → Reject H₀.
    4. P-value method: Φ(2.40) = 0.9918 → p = 1 − 0.9918 = 0.0082. Since 0.0082 < 0.05 → Reject H₀. Same decision.
    5. Interpretation: sufficient statistical evidence that the average server latency has increased. Actions: check traffic load, inspect database performance, check recent software deployment, increase server capacity.
    Case study 2New algorithm performance — left-tailed

    The old algorithm averages 10 s. A new optimization claims to reduce execution time. Given σ = 2, n = 100, x̄ = 9.5 s, α = 0.05.

    1. Hypotheses: H₀: μ = 10 vs H₁: μ < 10 (we want a decrease) → left-tailed Z-test.
    2. Statistic: SE = 2/√100 = 0.2; Z = (9.5 − 10)/0.2 = −2.5.
    3. Critical-value method: left-tailed critical value = −1.645. Since −2.5 < −1.645 → Reject H₀.
    4. P-value method: Φ(−2.50) = 0.0062 → p = 0.0062 < 0.05 → Reject H₀.
    5. Conclusion: sufficient evidence that the new algorithm reduces average execution time.
    Case study 3IoT sensor calibration — two-tailed

    A temperature sensor should average 25°C. The engineer wants to know whether calibration has changed — reading too high or too low both matter. Given σ = 2, n = 64, x̄ = 25.7°C, α = 0.05.

    1. Hypotheses: H₀: μ = 25 vs H₁: μ ≠ 25 → two-tailed Z-test.
    2. Statistic: SE = 2/√64 = 0.25; Z = (25.7 − 25)/0.25 = 0.7/0.25 = 2.8.
    3. Critical-value method: two-tailed critical values ±1.96. Since 2.8 > 1.96 → Reject H₀.
    4. P-value method: Φ(2.80) = 0.9974 → right-tail = 1 − 0.9974 = 0.0026 → p = 2 × 0.0026 = 0.0052 < 0.05 → Reject H₀.
    5. Conclusion: the sensor calibration appears to have changed significantly.
    Case study 4Cybersecurity login monitoring — right-tailed

    A security team normally observes an average of 50 failed login attempts per hour and wants to detect an increase (an increase could indicate brute-force attack, credential stuffing, or bot activity). Given σ = 15, n = 100, x̄ = 53, α = 0.05.

    1. Hypotheses: H₀: μ = 50 vs H₁: μ > 50 → right-tailed Z-test.
    2. Statistic: SE = 15/√100 = 1.5; Z = (53 − 50)/1.5 = 3/1.5 = 2.
    3. Critical-value method: critical value = 1.645. Since 2 > 1.645 → Reject H₀.
    4. P-value method: Φ(2.00) = 0.9772 → p = 1 − 0.9772 = 0.0228 < 0.05 → Reject H₀.
    5. Conclusion & action: sufficient evidence of an increase in failed login attempts. The SOC team may investigate IP addresses, enable rate limiting, trigger security alerts, check authentication logs.
    Case study 5Semiconductor manufacturing — two-tailed

    A microchip requires an average operating voltage of 5 V. A shift above or below 5 V both cause problems. Given σ = 0.2 V, n = 100, x̄ = 5.05 V, α = 0.05.

    1. Hypotheses: H₀: μ = 5 vs H₁: μ ≠ 5 → two-tailed Z-test.
    2. Statistic: SE = 0.2/√100 = 0.02; Z = (5.05 − 5)/0.02 = 0.05/0.02 = 2.5.
    3. Critical-value method: ±1.96. Since 2.5 > 1.96 → Reject H₀.
    4. P-value method: Φ(2.50) = 0.9938 → right tail = 0.0062 → p = 2 × 0.0062 = 0.0124 < 0.05 → Reject H₀.
    5. Conclusion: evidence that the manufacturing process mean has shifted. Important point: even a small physical change (5 V → 5.05 V) can be statistically significant when measurement variation is small and the sample size is large.
    Case study 6Battery improvement — right-tailed

    The current battery has an average life of 8 hours. A new design claims to improve battery life — we care specifically about an increase. Given σ = 1.5, n = 100, x̄ = 8.3 hours, α = 0.05.

    1. Hypotheses: H₀: μ = 8 vs H₁: μ > 8 → right-tailed Z-test.
    2. Statistic: SE = 1.5/√100 = 0.15; Z = (8.3 − 8)/0.15 = 0.3/0.15 = 2.
    3. Critical-value method: critical value = 1.645. Since 2 > 1.645 → Reject H₀.
    4. P-value method: p = 1 − Φ(2.00) = 1 − 0.9772 = 0.0228 < 0.05 → Reject H₀.

    4.10Statistical significance vs practical importance

    Caution exampleDatabase indexing technique

    A company tests whether a new database indexing technique improves query performance. Suppose p = 0.02 and α = 0.05: the result is statistically significant at the 5% level.

    But: if the improvement is only 0.01 milliseconds (p-value = 0.001), the result may be statistically significant yet have little practical value. Industry analysis should also consider: effect size · cost · performance improvement · business impact.

    Key insight

    Statistical significance does not automatically mean practical importance. A tiny effect can be “significant” with a large enough n; a valuable effect can be “non-significant” with too little data. Report the size of the effect, not just the verdict.

    The unit in one line

    Engineers deal with variation; statistics helps us decide whether that variation is acceptable: data → model → probability → engineering decision.

    A factory produces shafts with target μ = 50 mm, manufacturing variation σ = 0.02 mm, and customer tolerance 50 ± 0.05 mm (so 49.95 < X < 50.05). What percentage of shafts can we sell?

    5.4Normal approximation to the Binomial

    Until now we measured things — diameter, weight, volume, temperature. But engineers also count: number of defective chips, failed components, successful transmissions, faulty products, defective LEDs, packet errors.

    Quick recap — the counting distributions

Binomial: n fixed independent Bernoulli trials counting successes with the same p; P(X = x) = C(n, x)px(1−p)n−x. Example: 10 login attempts → number of successful logins. Memory: Bernoulli = 1 trial, Binomial = many Bernoulli trials.

    Worked exampleDefective components — the full approximation

    A factory produces 10,000 components; the probability a component is defective is 0.02. What is the probability that between 180 and 220 components are defective?

    1. Exact model: X ~ B(n = 10000, p = 0.02) — but computing P(180 ≤ X ≤ 220) directly from the binomial formula is cumbersome. Can we use the Normal approximation? Yes — under suitable conditions.
    2. The bridge: for X ~ B(n, p): mean μ = np = 10000 × 0.02 = 200; variance σ² = npq = 10000 × 0.02 × 0.98 = 196, so σ = 14 (approximately). So X ≈ N(200, 14²).
    3. Continuity correction (binomial is discrete, normal is continuous): P(180 ≤ X ≤ 220) becomes P(179.5 < Y < 220.5) — extend each boundary outward by 0.5.
    4. Standardise: Z₁ = (179.5 − 200)/14 = −1.46; Z₂ = (220.5 − 200)/14 = +1.46.
    5. Z-table: P(−1.46 < Z < 1.46) = 2Φ(1.46) − 1 ≈ 2(0.9279) − 1 ≈ 0.857.

    For the record: the exact binomial answer is 0.8571 — the approximation is excellent. (Binomial gives the mean and spread; the Normal gives the bell curve and area.)

    Key insight — why the correction works

    Discrete numbers become continuous intervals, so we move the boundaries by 0.5. Each binomial value k is represented by the interval k ± 0.5; when the interval [180, 220] is expressed in these continuous boundaries it becomes [179.5, 220.5].

    5.5Normal approximation to the Poisson

    Quick recap — Poisson distribution

    Models the number of events in a fixed interval of time/space, occurring independently at an average rate λ. X = 0, 1, 2, 3, … with PMF: P(X = x) = e−λλx/x!. Examples: customer requests per minute, network packets per second, cyber attacks per hour, software bugs per module, machine failures per month. Memory: Binomial → fixed number of trials; Poisson → fixed interval + count of events.

    Worked exampleServer requests per minute

    A server receives an average of 100 requests per minute: X ~ Poisson(λ = 100). What is the probability that it receives between 90 and 110 requests in one minute?

    1. For Poisson: μ = λ = 100 and σ = √λ = 10. So we approximate X ~ N(100, 10²).
    2. Apply the continuity correction: P(90 ≤ X ≤ 110) becomes P(89.5 < Y < 110.5).
    3. Standardise: Z₁ = (89.5 − 100)/10 = −1.05; Z₂ = (110.5 − 100)/10 = +1.05.
    4. Z-table: P(−1.05 < Z < 1.05) = 2Φ(1.05) − 1 ≈ 2(0.8531) − 1 ≈ 0.706.
    When is the approximation allowed? Poisson → Normal when λ ≥ 10  (larger values give a better approximation)

    Approximation is about practicality: it lets us replace an awkward exact computation with one table lookup, at negligible cost in accuracy when the conditions hold.

    6.1Master formula sheet

    Normal distribution

    X ~ N(μ, σ²) · PDF f(x) = (1/(σ√2π))·e−(x−μ)²/2σ²
    Mean = median = mode · symmetric · total area = 1 · P(X = a) = 0

    Empirical rule

    μ ± 1σ ≈ 68.27% · μ ± 2σ ≈ 95.45% · μ ± 3σ ≈ 99.73%

    Z-score

    Z = (X − μ)/σ  ·  Φ(0) = 0.5 · Φ(−z) = 1 − Φ(z)

    Probability rules

    P(Z < z) = Φ(z) · P(Z > z) = 1 − Φ(z) · P(a < Z < b) = Φ(b) − Φ(a)

    Sample statistics

    x̄ = Σxi/n · s² = Σ(xi − x̄)²/(n − 1) — divide by n − 1 (degrees of freedom)

    Standard errors

    SE(x̄) = σ/√n · SE(p̂) ≈ √(p̂(1−p̂)/n) · FPC: × √((N−n)/(N−1)) · n ×4 → SE ×½

    CLT

    For large n, x̄ ≈ Normal(μ, σ/√n) regardless of the population’s shape. 95% interval: x̄ ± 1.96·SE

    Confidence interval (mean)

    x̄ ± zα/2·σ/√n (σ known) · x̄ ± tα/2, n−1·s/√n (σ unknown, n < 30)

    Z-test for a mean

    Z = (x̄ − μ₀)/(σ/√n) — how many standard errors x̄ is from the claimed mean

    P-values

    Right: 1 − Φ(Z) · Left: Φ(Z) · Two: 2[1 − Φ(|Z|)] · Reject H₀ iff p < α

    Binomial → Normal

    X ~ B(n, p) ≈ N(np, npq) when np ≥ 5, nq ≥ 5 · continuity correction ±0.5

    Poisson → Normal

    X ~ Poisson(λ) ≈ N(λ, λ) — mean λ, SD √λ — when λ ≥ 10 · continuity correction ±0.5

    6.4The master list of traps and misconceptions

    Twenty ways this unit loses marks — read the night before
    1. P(X = a) = 0 for any continuous distribution — probability lives on intervals.
    2. The empirical rule applies only to normal data (and 68–95–99.7 are rounded).
    3. SD ≠ SE. SD = spread of data; SE = spread of the sample mean across repeated samples.
    4. Randomly collected ≠ randomly selected. Randomness must come from the selection mechanism.
    5. Sampling error is not a mistake — it is the inherent gap between statistic and parameter.
    6. Sample variance divides by n − 1 (one degree of freedom spent estimating x̄); population variance divides by N.
    7. Unbiased ≠ accurate every time — it is a long-run average property.
    8. Different samples give different statistics — that is sampling variability, not an error.
    9. CLT is about sample means, not data. The population never “becomes normal”.
    10. n = 30 is not a magic guarantee; CLT needs suitable conditions (no extreme outliers, independence).
    11. CLT reduces variability; it does not fix bias. Big biased samples are still biased.
    12. Big data ≠ unbiased data.
    13. Choosing Z vs T: σ unknown or n < 30 → t; σ known → z.
    14. “95% confident” describes the procedure’s long-run capture rate, not a probability that a fixed μ moves.
    15. Never “accept H₀” — always “fail to reject H₀” (and never say “acceptance region”).
    16. Type I = reject a true H₀ (false positive); Type II = keep a false H₀ (false negative). α controls Type I only.
    17. Two-tailed at α = 0.05 uses ±1.96; one-tailed uses ±1.645 — don’t swap them.
    18. Statistical significance ≠ practical importance (the 0.01 ms improvement).
    19. Continuity correction: adjust in the direction that widens the event — P(X ≤ 20) → Y < 20.5 but P(X < 20) → Y < 19.5.
    20. Login attempts are discrete (Poisson is the better model); the normal there is for standardization intuition. Trees (RF/XGBoost/LightGBM) don’t need normality at all.

    6.5The universal 10-step solver

    Almost every numerical problem in this unit yields to this sequence:

    1. Classify the variable: measured (continuous) → Normal; counted with fixed n → Binomial; counted per interval → Poisson.
    2. Check approximations: np ≥ 5 and nq ≥ 5 (Binomial) or λ ≥ 10 (Poisson) → switch to the Normal with the right μ and σ.
    3. Extract the givens: μ (or x̄), σ (or s), n, confidence level or α.
    4. Compute the standard error: SE = σ/√n (or √(p̂(1−p̂)/n) for proportions).
    5. Decide the task: estimate a range → CI; judge a claim → hypothesis test.
    6. For a CI: pick the critical value (z or t), compute x̄ ± critical × SE, state the interpretation.
    7. For a test: write H₀ and H₁, and read the direction of H₁ to fix the tail(s).
    8. Compute Z = (x̄ − μ₀)/SE and compare with the critical value(s) — or compute the P-value with the matching formula.
    9. Decide: reject H₀ if |Z| exceeds the critical value, equivalently if p < α; otherwise fail to reject.
    10. Interpret in engineering language — what should the company, admin, or SOC team now do?
