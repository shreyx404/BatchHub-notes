Data Communications

When we communicate, we are sharing information. That sharing can be local (face to face, between individuals) or remote (over a distance). The term telecommunication — which includes telephony, telegraphy and television — means communication at a distance (tele is Greek for “far”).

Definition · Data

Data refers to information presented in whatever form is agreed upon by the parties creating and using the data.

Definition · Data communications

Data communications is the exchange of data between two devices via some form of transmission medium, such as a wire cable. For it to occur, the communicating devices must be part of a communication system made up of a combination of hardware (physical equipment) and software (programs).

1.1The four fundamental characteristics

The effectiveness of a data-communication system is judged on exactly four grounds — remember them as D·A·T·J:

1 · Delivery
The system must deliver data to the correct destination — received by the intended device or user, and only by that device or user.
2 · Accuracy
The system must deliver data accurately. Data altered in transmission and left uncorrected are unusable.
3 · Timeliness
The system must deliver data in time. Data delivered late are useless. For video and audio, timely delivery means delivering data as they are produced, in the same order they are produced, and without significant delay — this is called real-time transmission.
4 · Jitter
Jitter is the variation in packet arrival time — the uneven delay in delivery of audio or video packets. If video packets are sent every 30 ms but some arrive after 30 ms and others after 40 ms, the result is uneven video quality.
Fig. 01 — Jitter. Sent at a steady 30 ms rhythm, packets land with alternating 30 / 40 ms delays. The uneven gaps are what you perceive as stuttering video or choppy audio.

1.2The five components

A data-communications system has five components — Sender & Receiver exchange a Message over a Medium using a Protocol:

Fig. 02 — The five components. Message + Sender + Receiver + Transmission medium + Protocol. Without the protocol, two devices may be connected yet unable to communicate — like a French speaker and a Japanese speaker on the same phone call.
1 · Message
The information (data) to be communicated — text, numbers, pictures, audio, video.
2 · Sender
The device that sends the data message — computer, workstation, telephone handset, video camera…
3 · Receiver
The device that receives the message — computer, workstation, telephone handset, television…
4 · Transmission medium
The physical path the message travels from sender to receiver — twisted-pair wire, coaxial cable, fiber-optic cable, radio waves.
5 · Protocol
A set of rules that governs data communications — an agreement between the communicating devices. Connection without a shared protocol is not communication.

1.3Worked real-world case — a WhatsApp message

Application example
  1. Your device → the internet. The message leaves via Wi-Fi (radio waves to your router) or mobile data (4G/5G radio to the nearest cell tower, then fiber/copper inside the carrier’s network).
  2. Across the internet (wired). It travels through routers, switches and servers — mostly over fiber-optic backbone (light signals) and copper (electrical signals) maintained by ISPs and data centres.
  3. WhatsApp servers. The message is end-to-end encrypted, then routed to WhatsApp’s cloud servers (Meta) — possibly in another country, depending on load balancing and routing.
  4. Server → recipient. The path reverses: server → recipient’s ISP → their phone via Wi-Fi or mobile data.

Data & Signals

Data is any digital information transmitted between devices over a network. It appears as text (emails, messages), numbers (sensor readings), images, audio and video (streaming media) and binary files (software, documents). Fundamentally, it comes in two flavours:

2.1Analog data — continuous

  • Can take any value within a given range — it varies smoothly over time, with no sudden jumps.
  • Represented by sine waves; used in traditional systems — telephone lines, radio.
  • Examples: the human voice converted to an electric signal in a microphone, temperature on a thermometer, analog TV / FM radio signals.

2.2Digital data — discrete

  • Has fixed, separate values — binary 0s and 1s — represented by square waves, like an on–off switch.
  • Easier to store, process and transmit with low noise.
  • Examples: text files, MP3 music, data sent over the internet, everything stored in your phone or computer.
Fig. 03 — Analog vs digital. The sine flows without breaks; the square wave jumps between exactly two levels — every value is unambiguous, which is why digital data resists noise.
Basis Analog data Digital data
Nature Continuous Discrete — fixed, separate values
Representation Sine waves Square waves (0s and 1s)
Values Any value within a range Only specific (binary) values
Noise behaviour Degrades easily Low noise; easy to store, process, transmit
Typical systems Telephone lines, radio, analog TV/FM Computers, the internet, MP3, storage
03

Protocols, Standards & Standard Organizations

Definition · Protocol

A set of rules that defines how data is formatted, transmitted and received — e.g. TCP/IP, HTTP, FTP. It is the agreement that lets two different machines understand each other.

Go deeper — the three key elements of a protocol
Syntax
Structure / format of the data — the order in which fields are presented (e.g. a protocol may expect the first 8 bits to be the sender’s address).
Semantics
The meaning of each section of bits — how a particular pattern is to be interpreted and what action to take (e.g. does an address identify the final destination, or the next hop?).
Timing
When data should be sent, and how fast — if the sender pushes data at 100 Mbps but the receiver can only process 1 Mbps, the link overloads and data is lost.
Definition · Standards

Standards are documented agreements containing technical specifications or other precise criteria, used consistently as rules/guidelines to ensure that: devices and systems can interoperate; reliability, efficiency and compatibility are achieved; and data is communicated in a predictable, error-free manner. They provide consistency among hardware and software from different vendors.

Two categories of standards

De facto (“by fact”) — standards that have emerged by widespread use/convention, not by formal approval (e.g. TCP/IP, HTTP, the IBM PC architecture). De jure (“by law”) — standards ratified by an officially recognized body through a formal process (e.g. ISO standards, the IEEE 802 series).

Data Flow — Modes of Communication

Communication between two devices can be simplex, half-duplex or full-duplex. Watch the travelling dots in the diagram — they show exactly who may speak, and when:

Fig. 04 — Data-flow modes. One-way street · one-lane road with two-way traffic · two-way street. The moving dots are live — reload the page to restart them.

4.1Simplex

  • Communication is unidirectional, like a one-way street: only one of the two devices on a link can transmit; the other can only receive.
  • Examples: keyboards (only introduce input) and traditional monitors (only accept output).
  • The entire capacity of the channel is available for that one direction.

4.2Half-duplex

  • Each station can both transmit and receive — but not at the same time: when one device is sending, the other can only receive, and vice versa.
  • Like a one-lane road with traffic allowed both ways — cars in one direction make the other direction wait.
  • The entire channel capacity is taken over by whichever device is transmitting at the time.
  • Examples: walkie-talkies and CB (citizens band) radios — a ~27 MHz (11 m band) short-range two-way radio; you press-to-talk, release to listen, and only one person can speak on a channel at a time.
  • Used where simultaneous two-way communication is unnecessary — then each direction can exploit the whole capacity in turn.

4.3Full-duplex (duplex)

  • Both stations transmit and receive simultaneously — a two-way street with traffic flowing both ways at once.
  • Sharing happens in one of two ways: the link contains two physically separate transmission paths (one for sending, one for receiving), or the capacity of the channel is divided between the two directions.
  • Example: the telephone network — both people can talk and listen at the same time.
  • Used when communication in both directions is required all the time; the channel capacity, however, must be divided between the two directions.
Mode Direction Analogy Channel capacity Examples
Simplex One-way only One-way street Full capacity in one direction Keyboard, monitor, broadcast TV
Half-duplex Both ways, one at a time One-lane road, two-way traffic Whole channel owned by current sender Walkie-talkie, CB radio
Full-duplex Both ways simultaneously Two-way street Two separate paths, or divided between directions Telephone network

05

Bandwidth

Definition · Bandwidth

Bandwidth is the maximum rate at which data can be transmitted over a communication channel in a given amount of time — the maximum data capacity of a network path. If a network’s bandwidth is 40 Mbps, it cannot transmit data faster than 40 Mbps under any circumstance.

Bandwidth is often mistaken for internet speed. It is actually the volume of information that can be sent over a connection in a measured amount of time, calculated in megabits per second (Mbps). In one line: bandwidth is how much information you receive every second; speed is how fast that information is received or downloaded.

Bandwidth vs frequency: frequency and bandwidth are closely related, but bandwidth is not frequency. The frequency spectrum a signal occupies is its bandwidth: bandwidth = difference between the upper and lower frequency components of a signal. This is how the amount of data delivered per second is specified.

Network speed refers to the actual rate at which data is transmitted. It depends on the protocols used, a device’s wired/wireless reception capacity, and a server’s ability to serve multiple clients — and low bandwidth caps it.

Fig. 05 — The highway analogy. More lanes (higher bandwidth) → more cars (data) pass per second. Fewer lanes → congestion. The cars’ speed is a separate question — that’s why bandwidth ≠ speed.

5.1Units and types

  • Digital bandwidth is measured in bits per second (bps) and multiples: Kbps (10³ bps), Mbps (10⁶ bps), Gbps (10⁹ bps).
  • Analog bandwidth is measured in hertz (Hz) — the range of frequencies a channel can carry (fmax − fmin). Digital bandwidth is measured in bps — the quantity of data per unit time.
formula Bandwidth (bps) = Baud Rate × log2(L) Baud rate = symbol rate (signal changes per second) · L = number of signal levels
worked examplebandwidth from baud
  1. Baud rate = 1200 symbols/s, L = 2 levels → log₂2 = 1 → Bandwidth = 1200 × 1 = 1200 bps (bps equals baud when there are only two levels).
  2. Baud rate = 1200 symbols/s, L = 16 levels → log₂16 = 4 → Bandwidth = 1200 × 4 = 4800 bps — each signal change now carries 4 bits.

5.2Factors affecting bandwidth

  • Transmission medium — fiber optics offer far higher bandwidth than copper.
  • Signal type — analog signals have a limited frequency range.
  • Noise & interference — reduce the effective data rate.
  • Hardware capabilities — routers, switches, NICs.
  • Protocols used — some introduce overhead (headers, acknowledgements).

5.3How to increase bandwidth

  • ISPs / internet plan — take a higher-Mbps plan when you stream heavily and run many connected devices.
  • Router — upgrade to a current router supporting faster, higher frequencies.
  • Wired network — connect computers to the router with an Ethernet cable; being wired avoids congestion on the airwaves and prevents bandwidth/connection issues caused by other devices.
likely exam questions
  • Define bandwidth. Distinguish bandwidth, network speed and frequency.
  • A channel has baud rate 1000 and 8 signal levels — find the bandwidth in bps. (1000 × 3 = 3000 bps)

Definition · Data transmission

Data transmission refers to the methods of transferring data over a communications medium from one device to another. Transmission may be analog or digital, but modern networking is concerned with digital transmission — the sending and receiving of data as a digital bit stream.

Data Transmission Rate

Definition · DTR

The data transfer rate (DTR) is the amount of digital data moved from one place to another in a given time — the speed of travel of a given amount of data. The greater the bandwidth of a path, the higher the DTR. The DTR is also called throughput.

formula DTR = D / T D = total digital data transferred · T = total time taken · measured in bps (Kbps · Mbps · Gbps)
worked exampleDTR of a file transfer
  1. Convert time to seconds. A 100 Mb transfer completes in 2 minutes → T = 2 × 60 = 120 s.
  2. Apply the formula. DTR = D / T = 100 / 120 ≈ 0.83 Mbps.

Practice: 4 MB (= 32 Mb) moved in 8 s → DTR = 32 / 8 = 4 Mbps.

7.1Typical rates in the wild

  • A low-speed internet connection: 33.6 Kbps.
  • Gigabit Ethernet LAN: up to 1,000 Mbps.
  • Modern switches reach the terabit range — e.g. Cisco’s Silicon One G100: up to 25.6 Tbps.
  • USB shows the medium’s effect: USB 1.0 = 12 Mbps vs USB 2.0 = 480 Mbps.
Analogy

Think of the data transmission rate like the speed of a car: a higher speed means the car (data) reaches its destination sooner. Likewise a higher data rate means the bits reach the receiver faster.

How is DTR tested? Online tools (e.g. Speedtest by Ookla) measure a connection against geographically dispersed servers — checking download speed, upload speed and latency. Users mostly run them to verify that the ISP delivers the promised speed.

7.2What affects data transfer rates — the seven factors

1 · Network congestion
Traffic reaches network capacity → drop in quality of service (QoS): packet loss, queuing delays, blocking of new connections. Users see slow transfers, sluggish internet, session timeouts and higher time-to-live in ping tests.
2 · Network latency
Total time a packet takes from source to destination. Depends on the number of hops, performance of routers/switches, physical distance, and the protocol: UDP barely affects latency (connectionless), while TCP does (connection-oriented, requires acknowledgements).
3 · Inadequate hardware resources
A client or server short on processing power, disk, I/O or RAM slows the whole network’s DTR — queries and standard TCP transfers stall.
4 · Filtering & encryption
Firewalls and filters consume processing time scanning traffic; excessive scanning generates packet loss and retransmissions, slowing source→destination transfer.
5 · Traffic prioritization
Also called class of service — traffic is classified high/medium/low. Critical, time-sensitive apps (VoIP, video conferencing) get guaranteed uplink bandwidth; under congestion, low-priority traffic suffers loss, disconnects and slow transfers.
6 · Load balancing
Distributes traffic across a pool of servers. Misconfigured load balancers cause packet loss, slower transmission, retransmissions.
7 · Transmission medium
DTR differs per medium — optical fiber ≠ twisted pair; USB generations differ (12 vs 480 Mbps).
likely exam questions
  • Define DTR/throughput. A 600 Mb file transfers in 3 minutes — find the DTR. (600/180 ≈ 3.33 Mbps)
  • List and explain any five factors that affect data transfer rates.

Baud Rate vs Bits per Second

Both measure transmission “speed”, but they are not the same thing — a classic trap question.

Bits per second (bps)
The number of bits (0 or 1) transmitted per second — how much useful data is sent each second. Units: Kbps (10³), Mbps (10⁶), Gbps (10⁹). This is the actual measure of digital data per second; higher bps = faster transmission. If 1000 bits are transmitted in one second, the speed is 1000 bps.
Baud rate
The number of signal units (symbols) transmitted per second — how many times the signal changes (modulates) per second. A symbol may represent one or more bits, depending on the modulation technique. Unit: baud (symbols/second).
formula bps = Baud Rate × Number of bits per symbol In modern communication (DSL, Wi-Fi…), modulation packs several bits into each symbol, so bps is usually higher than baud
worked examplebps from baud
  1. A line changes signal 2400 times per second (baud = 2400) and each symbol encodes 4 bits → bps = 2400 × 4 = 9600 bps.
  2. If each symbol encodes only 1 bit (two signal levels), bps = baud — the two measures coincide.
Basis bps (bit rate) Baud rate
Measures Bits transmitted per second Signal units (symbols) per second
Concerns Amount of useful digital data Number of signal changes per second
Unit bps / Kbps / Mbps / Gbps Baud (symbols per second)
Relation bps = baud × bits per symbol · bps ≥ baud · equal only for 1 bit per symbol

Types of Errors

Definition · Error

An error is the condition when the receiver’s information does not match the sender’s. Digital signals suffer from noise during transmission, which can flip binary bits: a 0 may change to 1, or a 1 to 0. Data — handled at the Data Link layer or Transport layer of the OSI model — may get scrambled or corrupted in transit. To counter this, error-detection codes are added as extra (redundant) data to digital messages.

9.1Single-bit error

Exactly one bit of the whole data packet is changed, corrupted or altered — typically due to induced noise in the cable or electromagnetic interference. It sounds small and harmless, but it can corrupt the entire decoded data:

sent0111decimal 7
received0011decimal 3 — one flipped bit
Fig. 07 — Single-bit error. 0111 (7) becomes 0011 (3). When this feeds into complex logic, huge data corruption follows. Detection needs a worthy mechanism — CRC, parity bits, etc.

9.2Multiple-bit error

More than one bit is affected, at random, non-consecutive positions. It is relatively rare compared to single-bit errors, but occurs in high-noise / high-interference environments.

9.3Burst error

Multiple data bits are corrupted within a very short span of time — hence “burst”. Main causes: communication-line interference and impulsive noise. A burst can fully corrupt a packet and make it valueless. Retransmission fixes it but consumes network resources (and the retransmission can itself suffer a burst), so robust codes — Reed–Solomon, convolutional codes — add redundancy from which the receiver can reconstruct the original data.

multiple-bit100011scattered flips — random noise
burst101101clustered flips — impulsive noise (3 bits at once)
Fig. 08 — Multiple-bit vs burst. Sent 110010 → received 010011 (scattered) · sent 110001 → received 101101 (clustered burst of 3).
Feature Multiple-bit error Burst error
Bit positions Random / non-consecutive Consecutive / adjacent
Cause Random noise Temporary disturbance (impulsive noise, line interference)
Error location Spread across the data Clustered in a small region
Detection complexity Easier to isolate Harder to detect and correct
Go deeper — where do single-bit vs burst errors actually occur?

In serial transmission one bit is on the line per unit time, so a short noise spike lasting several bit-times corrupts several consecutive bits — a burst. In parallel transmission all bits travel simultaneously on separate wires, so one noisy wire flips exactly one bit position — a single-bit error. This is why burst errors dominate serial links and single-bit errors are associated with parallel ones.

likely exam questions

10

Redundancy

Definition · Redundancy

Redundancy is the duplication of critical components or functions of a system with the intention of increasing reliability and availability. If one part fails, the redundant part takes over — ensuring minimal or no disruption. In error control, the “extra” redundancy bits are exactly what makes detection possible.

10.1 Data redundancy

Storing the same data in multiple locations/formats so data is never lost to corruption or deletion. Examples: database replication across servers; backups on cloud + physical drives; RAID (Redundant Array of Independent Disks).

10.2 Path (network) redundancy

Multiple communication paths between devices — if one link/node fails, data still arrives via another path. Examples: switches running Spanning Tree Protocol; dual internet connections (load balancing / failover); mesh topology.

10.3 Hardware redundancy

Duplicating physical components to survive hardware failure. Examples: dual power supplies in servers; mirrored hard drives; redundant routers/switches in a data centre.

10.4 Software redundancy

Multiple software systems or backup code modules that detect and recover from software failures. Examples: multiple antivirus engines; recovery programs for corrupted applications; redundant services running in parallel.

Advantages of redundancy Disadvantages of redundancy
Increases reliability and availability Increases cost (hardware, storage, bandwidth)
Improves fault tolerance Introduces complexity in system design
Helps in error detection and correction Requires additional management and maintenance
Ensures continuous service in critical systems —
Use cases

Data centres — multiple ISPs, power sources, servers. Cloud computing — replication across regions/zones. Banking & military systems — high availability and disaster recovery.

11

Error Detection — VRC · LRC · Checksum · CRC

Errors occur from noise, attenuation, interference or other disruptions. Error-detection techniques identify whether the received data is wrong — they do not correct it (unless paired with a correction scheme). The common idea in all of them: introduce redundancy bits that carry extra information about the data. Four methods, from simplest to strongest: VRC → LRC → Checksum → CRC.

11.1Vertical Redundancy Check (VRC) — simple parity

A simple error-detection method that adds one extra bit (the parity bit) to each data unit (e.g. each byte), checking each unit vertically. It is a 1-D parity check — parity is computed only per row/byte. Rule (even parity):

  • 1 is added to the block if it contains an odd number of 1s,
  • 0 is added if it contains an even number of 1s — making the total number of 1s even (hence “even parity checking”).
worked exampleeven & odd parity

Data = 1011001 (7 bits) — the number of 1s = 4 (even):

even parity10110010parity bit = 0
odd parity10110011parity bit = 1
failure caseeven number of errors slips through

Data to be transmitted = 101010 → even-parity codeword sent = 1010101. Suppose two bits flip in transit:

sent1010101
received1111101

The receiver counts the 1s — still even — and wrongly concludes “no error”. That is VRC’s blind spot.

advantages
  • Detects all single-bit errors and any odd number of errors.
  • Trivially easy to implement in hardware and software.
  • Minimal extra data — only one bit per data unit.
  • Fast — calculating/checking is essentially instant.
disadvantages
  • Cannot detect an even number of bit errors.
  • No error-correction capability.
  • Inefficient for large data blocks; no protection against burst errors.

11.2Longitudinal Redundancy Check (LRC) — two-dimensional parity

Parity bits are calculated for each row (equivalent to a simple parity/VRC bit per row) and for all columns; both are sent along with the data. At the receiver, the same parities are recomputed and compared. Checking rows and columns dramatically raises the chance of catching multiple errors.

worked examplebuilding the LRC block
c1 c2 c3 c4 c5 c6 c7 c8 VRC
R1 1 1 0 0 1 0 1 0 0
R2 0 1 1 0 1 1 0 0 0
R3 1 0 1 1 0 1 0 1 1
R4 0 1 0 1 1 0 1 0 0
LRC 0 1 0 0 1 0 0 1 1

Every row gets its even-parity VRC bit (column 9); every column gets its even-parity bit (the LRC row) — including a parity bit for the VRC column itself. The whole 5 × 9 block is transmitted.

why LRC is strongerdetecting & locating a single-bit error
c1 c2 c3 c4 c5 c6 c7 c8 VRC
R1 1 1 0 0 1 0 1 0 0
R2 0 1 1 0 1 1 0 0 0
R3 1 0 1 1 1 1 0 1 1
R4 0 1 0 1 1 0 1 0 0
LRC 0 1 0 0 1 0 0 1 1

Bit R3·c5 flips in transit (filled cell). Now row 3’s parity fails (boxed VRC cell) and column 5’s parity fails (boxed LRC cell). The intersection of the failing row and failing column pinpoints the exact bit — so a single-bit error can be corrected by flipping it back, not merely detected. Two- or three-bit errors anywhere in the matrix are detected too (though not corrected).

advantages
  • Detects and corrects all single-bit errors (row × column intersection).
  • Detects two- and three-bit errors anywhere in the matrix.
  • Easy to implement — XOR logic / simple 1-counting; no polynomials, no heavy computation.
disadvantages
  • Cannot correct two- or three-bit errors — detection only.
  • If an even number of bits flips in the same column, it goes undetected (like VRC).
  • Extra row ⇒ overhead; inefficient for small data.
  • Unsuitable for high-noise / burst-heavy channels.
  • LRC alone (without per-row VRC) can detect an error but cannot say which bit is wrong — data bit or parity bit.

11.3Checksum

Checksum error detection identifies errors using 1’s-complement addition:

sender
  1. Divide the data into k segments, each of m bits.
  2. Add the segments using 1’s complement arithmetic to get the sum.
  3. Complement the sum — that is the checksum.
  4. Send the checksum segment along with the data segments.
receiver
  1. Add all received segments (data + checksum) using 1’s complement arithmetic.
  2. Complement the result.
  3. All zeroes ⇒ accept; anything else ⇒ discard.
worked examplechecksum with 8-bit segments
SENDER RECEIVER ────── ──────── 10011001 10011001 ⊕ 11100010 ⊕ 11100010 ⊕ 10100101 ⊕ 10100101 ⊕ 00111001 ⊕ 00111001 ───────── ⊕ 10100100 ← checksum 01011011 sum ───────── 10100100 checksum (1’s complement) 11111111 sum 00000000 complement = 0 ⇒ ACCEPT

1’s-complement arithmetic in action (end-around carry — any carry beyond 8 bits wraps around and is added back): 10011001 + 11100010 = 1 01111011 → wrap → 01111100; + 10100101 → wrap → 00100010; + 00111001 → 01011011. Complement → checksum 10100100. At the receiver, the five words total 11111111, whose complement is 00000000 — the data is accepted.

11.4Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC)

CRC is an error-detecting code used in digital networks and storage devices to detect accidental changes to raw data. Unlike the checksum (based on addition), CRC is based on binary division (modulo-2 / XOR). A sequence of redundant bits — the CRC bits — is appended to the data unit so the result is exactly divisible by a predetermined binary number (the generator polynomial). At the destination the incoming data unit is divided by the same number: no remainder ⇒ accept; a remainder ⇒ damaged in transit ⇒ reject.

procedure append (k−1) zeros → divide modulo-2 → remainder = CRC → codeword = data + CRC Data word length n · divisor length k · CRC has k−1 bits · codeword length = n + k − 1 bits
DATA 11010011 (n = 8) · GENERATOR 1011 = x³+x+1 (k = 4) · APPEND k−1 = 3 ZEROS
11010011 · 000

Dividend = 11010011 with three zeros appended → 11010011000. Step through the modulo-2 division — each step is one XOR of the 4-bit window with the generator.

setup
See the whole division at a glance
11010011000 ÷ 1011 (modulo-2: XOR, no carries) 1101 ⊕ 1011 = 0110 → 01100011000 1100 ⊕ 1011 = 0111 → 00111011000 1110 ⊕ 1011 = 0101 → 00010111000 1011 ⊕ 1011 = 0000 → 00000001000 1000 ⊕ 1011 = 0011 → 00000000011 remainder = 011 ⇒ CRC = 011 codeword = 11010011 011 = 11010011011 (n + k − 1 = 11 bits) receiver: 11010011011 ÷ 1011 → remainder 000 ⇒ accept

Rule of the window: find the leftmost 1 in the dividend, align the generator under the four bits starting there, XOR, and repeat until no 1 remains before the final (k−1) bits. Whatever is left at the end is the remainder — the CRC.

11.4.1Requirements for a good generator polynomial

  • Binary polynomial — represented in binary; e.g. 1011 means x³ + x + 1.
  • Highest and lowest bits must be 1 — the polynomial must start and end with 1. 1001 ✓; 0101 or 110 ✗ (invalid).
  • Chosen for the application — standard generators are the product of years of research to maximise detection of specific error types (bursts, odd numbers of errors…).
  • Not random — a randomly chosen polynomial may miss certain error patterns and reduce reliability.
advantages of CRC
  • High detection power: single-bit, double-bit, odd-numbered errors, and burst errors up to the length of the generator polynomial.
  • Efficient computation — pure XOR (modulo-2), fast in hardware and software.
  • Scalable and flexible — choose the generator per requirement.
  • Widely used: Ethernet, USB, storage, modems, wireless.
  • Suitable for large data blocks (vs parity/checksum).
  • Simple hardware — shift registers + XOR gates.
disadvantages of CRC
  • No error correction — detection only; a separate mechanism must recover.
  • Detection power depends heavily on the generator polynomial chosen.
  • Not cryptographic — no defence against deliberate tampering.
  • Overhead for small messages (redundant bits on low-bandwidth links).
  • Certain error patterns matching the generator’s structure slip through.

11.5Error detection — overall assessment

advantages
  • Increased data reliability — the recipient receives exactly what was sent.
  • Improved network performance — helps identify and isolate issues causing errors, reducing downtime.
  • Enhanced security — data that has been tampered with is flagged.
disadvantages
  • Overhead — extra resources and processing → slower performance, added latency.
  • False positives — unnecessary retransmissions, further loading the network.
  • Limited correction — detection alone can’t fix data; the sender must retransmit, adding delay and overhead.
Method Basis Redundancy Detects Corrects Complexity
VRC (simple parity) Parity per data unit 1 bit per unit All single-bit & odd-numbered errors No Trivial
LRC (2-D parity) Row + column parity 1 extra row (+ per-row VRC) Single-bit (and locates it), 2–3 bit errors Single-bit only Low
Checksum 1’s complement addition 1 segment Most errors (sum changes) No Moderate
CRC Modulo-2 binary division k − 1 bits Single, double, odd, bursts ≤ degree of generator No Higher (shift registers)

IEEE 802 Standards

Definition · IEEE 802

IEEE 802 is a family of networking standards developed by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, covering the Physical Layer (Layer 1) and the Data Link Layer (Layer 2) of the OSI model for LANs and MANs. They guarantee interoperability between devices from different vendors.

Fig. 09 — The 802 family. 802.2 (LLC) sits above every MAC; each technology defines its own MAC + PHY; 802.1 cuts across the whole stack managing and interconnecting it.

12.1IEEE 802.1 — architecture, bridging, management, security

802.1 deals with network management, bridging (switching), security and the overall architecture of LAN/MAN systems, mainly at Layer 2. Rather than defining a network type, it defines how networks are managed, interconnected and secured — the overall rulebook for how data travels across networks defined by the other 802 standards. Key features:

  • Spanning Tree Protocol (STP) — loop-free bridging.
  • VLANs — virtual bridged LANs.
  • LAN security — 802.1X (port-based access control).
  • QoS — quality of service: prioritising traffic types, controlling bandwidth, reducing latency and packet loss so voice/video/real-time data survive congestion.
  • Also: overall addressing, routing and management architecture.
Worked mini-case — PC sends a file to a printer
  1. Data packaging (LLC sublayer). LLC takes the PC’s data and wraps it into a frame — the box that holds the data — writing the destination MAC address (the printer’s “mailing address”) on the front.
  2. Addressing & sending (MAC sublayer). MAC adds the sender PC’s MAC address (the return address) and regulates how the frame gets onto the network cable — the post-office worker putting the box on the right truck.
  3. Specific delivery. The actual transmission is handled by a specific standard — in wired networks IEEE 802.3 (Ethernet) — which carries the frame over the cable. Real implementations involve more complex protocols depending on the network type.

12.2IEEE 802.2 — Logical Link Control (LLC)

802.2 defines the LLC sublayer — the upper sublayer of the Data Link Layer. While other 802 standards define MAC and physical layers, 802.2 defines how devices communicate reliably and manage errors above the MAC, giving the network layer a uniform interface regardless of the underlying technology (Ethernet, Token Ring, Wi-Fi…). LLC provides three modes of operation:

  • Type 1 — Unacknowledged connectionless service: frames are sent with no connection setup and no acknowledgements; error recovery is left to higher layers. Fast, low-overhead.
  • Type 2 — Connection-oriented service: connection is established, frames are sequenced and acknowledged, errors are recovered — reliable but heavier.
  • Type 3 — Acknowledged connectionless service: no connection setup, but each frame is individually acknowledged — a middle ground (used in factory-style environments).

12.3IEEE 802.3 — Ethernet

The most widely used standard for wired LANs. It defines the electrical and logical specifications for data transmission over coaxial, twisted-pair and fiber-optic cable, at speeds of 10 Mbps, 100 Mbps, 1 Gbps, 10 Gbps and 40 Gbps. Ethernet spans two layers that work together:

  • Physical layer (PHY) — electrical/optical characteristics: cable type, voltage levels, how data is encoded as electrical or light pulses.
  • MAC sublayer — manages sharing of the cable: frame format, MAC addressing, and the media access protocol CSMA/CD (Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection) to avoid collisions when multiple devices transmit simultaneously (in older, shared-medium versions).
Benefits of IEEE 802.3

Simple, mature and cheap to deploy · wide speed range (10 Mbps → 40 Gbps) · works over multiple media · dominant interoperability — nearly every wired device speaks it · modern switched, full-duplex operation has eliminated collisions entirely.

12.4IEEE 802.4 — Token Bus

802.4 defines the Token Bus protocol — now largely obsolete, but historically significant in early industrial and factory automation (General Motors’ MAP — Manufacturing Automation Protocol). Devices connect on a physical bus, but communicate in a logical ring: a special control frame, the token, passes from station to station in a predefined logical order, and only the token-holder may transmit.

Fig. 10 — Token Bus (802.4). One token (T) at a time ⇒ no collisions; every device gets a fair, predictable turn — ideal for real-time systems like assembly lines. Today it is obsolete: Ethernet won on simplicity, speed and cost.

12.5IEEE 802.5 — Token Ring

Token Ring was developed by IBM in the 1980s and later standardized as 802.5. Unlike Ethernet’s CSMA/CD, it uses token passing for media access (classic speeds: 4 and 16 Mbps).

  • Token-passing mechanism: a special token frame circulates continuously; only the station possessing the token may transmit; after sending, it passes the token to the next logical station.
  • Logical ring topology: devices form a logical ring even when physically wired as a star through an MSAU (Multi-Station Access Unit); data travels in one direction around the ring.
  • Frame types: token frame (controls access) and data frame (carries data) — the receiver copies the data and marks it received; the sender removes the frame when it returns.
  • Lifecycle: idle network → token circulates → a station seizes the token and sends → data circulates, receiver copies it, sender removes it → token regenerated and passed on.
  • History: used in IBM enterprises, banking and industrial systems in the 80s–90s; obsolete today — replaced by Ethernet on cost, speed and simplicity.
Fig. 11 — Token Ring (802.5). Stations form a logical ring (often star-wired through an MSAU); the black token square is animated, circling the ring forever.

12.6IEEE 802.11 — Wireless LANs (Wi-Fi)

802.11 defines the protocols and services for Wireless LANs (WLANs) — commonly known as Wi-Fi — using radio waves to connect devices. The family in brief:

Standard Band Max data rate Key technique
802.11 (legacy) 2.4 GHz 1–2 Mbps DSSS / FHSS
802.11a 5 GHz 54 Mbps OFDM
802.11b 2.4 GHz 11 Mbps DSSS
802.11g 2.4 GHz 54 Mbps OFDM
802.11n (Wi-Fi 4) 2.4 / 5 GHz up to 600 Mbps MIMO + channel bonding
802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5) 5 GHz multi-Gbps MU-MIMO, beamforming, wide channels

12.6.1Key technologies

  • OFDM — Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing: splits the signal across many sub-carriers for efficient, interference-resistant transmission.
  • MIMO — Multiple Input Multiple Output: multiple antennas send parallel streams to raise speed.
  • MU-MIMO — Multi-User MIMO: serves several devices simultaneously.
  • Beamforming — directs the signal toward the device instead of broadcasting everywhere.
  • Channel bonding — combines adjacent channels for higher throughput.

12.6.2802.11 architecture — BSS, AP, DS, ESS

Fig. 12 — 802.11 architecture. A BSS (Basic Service Set) is a cell of stations around an AP (Access Point); APs interconnect through the DS (Distribution System) to form an ESS. In ad-hoc mode, stations talk peer-to-peer with no AP.
802.11 MAC in one breath

Radio stations cannot detect collisions while transmitting, so 802.11 uses CSMA/CA — Collision Avoidance (listen before talk, interframe spacing, random back-off, optional RTS/CTS handshake against the hidden-terminal problem) instead of Ethernet’s CSMA/CD.

advantages
  • Wireless mobility and roaming capability
  • Easy deployment — no cabling
  • Scalable architecture
  • Adopted worldwide
limitations
  • Susceptible to interference (especially the crowded 2.4 GHz band)
  • Lower speed and range vs wired networks
  • Security concerns unless properly encrypted

The MAC Sublayer

The MAC (Medium Access Control) sublayer is the lower half of the Data Link Layer (Layer 2), sitting beneath the LLC. It controls how devices uniquely access the shared communication medium — wired or wireless. Without it, multiple devices on the same network would collide and corrupt each other’s data. It ensures data is framed, addressed, error-checked and safely transmitted.

13.1Main functions

  • Framing — encapsulates packets from LLC into frames, with header/trailer.
  • Physical addressing — adds source & destination MAC addresses to every frame.
  • Error detection — computes a CRC / FCS over the frame; receiver discards damaged frames.
  • Medium access control — arbitrates the shared channel: CSMA/CD (Ethernet 802.3), token passing (802.4 / 802.5), CSMA/CA (Wi-Fi 802.11).
  • Flow control & synchronization — paces transmission and works with the Physical Layer to move raw bits.
Technology Standard Access method
Ethernet IEEE 802.3 CSMA/CD
Wi-Fi IEEE 802.11 CSMA/CA
Token Ring IEEE 802.5 Token passing
Token Bus IEEE 802.4 Token passing

13.2The MAC address

A MAC address is a 48-bit hardware identifier, usually written as six hex pairs (e.g. 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E):

001A2B
OUI — organization, 24 bits
3C4D5E
device-specific, 24 bits
  • First 24 bits — OUI (Organizationally Unique Identifier): assigned to the manufacturer.
  • Last 24 bits — device-specific identifier: unique per interface the manufacturer produces.
  • Hardware-level — implemented in the NIC (Network Interface Card); works closely with the Physical Layer; handles collision resolution and synchronization.

14

Network Topologies

Mesh. n = 5 → 10 links

Mesh

Every device connects to every other device — full mesh (all nodes interconnected) or partial mesh (some nodes). A full mesh of n nodes needs n(n−1)/2 links, and each node needs n−1 interfaces (for n = 5: 10 links, 4 ports each).

Advantages: highly reliable and fault-tolerant — no data loss if one link fails; dedicated links mean no traffic conflicts. Drawbacks: costly and complex; difficult to install, manage and cable.

Star. all traffic via the hub

Star

All devices connect to a central device (hub or switch); all data flows through it.

Advantages: easy to add or remove devices; simple to manage and troubleshoot; a faulty cable affects only one node. Drawbacks: the central device is a single point of failure — hub down, network down; needs more cabling than bus.

Bus. one backbone, terminated at both ends

Bus

All devices tap into a single central cable (the bus). Data is broadcast — it travels in both directions until it reaches the destination (terminators absorb it at the ends).

Advantages: easy to install; requires the least cable. Drawbacks: difficult to troubleshoot; collisions are common; limited scalability; one backbone break kills the network.

Ring. each node joins exactly two neighbours

Ring

Each device connects to exactly two others, forming a circular loop. Data flow is typically unidirectional (or bidirectional), hopping node to node until it reaches its destination, which copies or removes it.

Advantages: reduced chance of collisions; predictable, orderly transmission — every station gets its turn. Drawbacks: troubleshooting is complex; slower due to hopping through nodes; a single break can disrupt the loop.

Topology Structure Failure impact Cable cost Best for
Mesh Every node ↔ every node — n(n−1)/2 links Minimal — routes around failures Highest Critical, fault-tolerant backbones
Star All nodes → central hub/switch Central device = single point of failure Moderate Offices, home networks (default choice)
Bus Single backbone, broadcast Backbone break stops all; collisions common Lowest Small, temporary networks
Ring Circular chain of two-neighbour nodes One break can disrupt the loop Low Orderly, deterministic access (Token Ring heritage)

15.1Formula sheet

DTR = D / T (also called throughput) Bandwidth (bps) = Baud Rate × log₂(L) (L = number of signal levels) bps = Baud Rate × bits per symbol (bps = baud only if 1 bit/symbol) CRC bits = k − 1 (k = length of the generator) Codeword length = n + k − 1 (n = data length) Full-mesh links = n(n−1)/2 interfaces per node = n − 1 MAC address = 48 bits = 24-bit OUI + 24-bit device ID

15.2Eighteen one-liners

  1. Data communication = exchange of data between two devices via a transmission medium (a system of hardware + software).
  2. Effectiveness = delivery, accuracy, timeliness, jitter (D·A·T·J).
  3. Components = message, sender, receiver, transmission medium, protocol.
  4. Simplex = one-way (keyboard/monitor) · half-duplex = alternating (walkie-talkie, CB) · full-duplex = simultaneous (telephone).
  5. Bandwidth = maximum data a channel can carry per second (bps) — the volume, not the speed; for a signal, bandwidth = upper − lower frequency.
  6. DTR = D/T, measured in bps; also called throughput.
  7. bps = baud × bits per symbol; baud = signal changes per second.
  8. Parallel transmission = bits side-by-side, short distance (printer); serial = one bit at a time, long distance (networks), framed by start/stop bits.
  9. Single-bit error: one bit flipped (0111 → 0011: 7 becomes 3). Multiple-bit: scattered flips. Burst: clustered flips in a short time — impulsive noise.
  10. VRC adds one parity bit; detects single-bit and odd-numbered errors; blind to even-numbered errors.
  11. LRC adds row + column parity; detects 2–3 bit errors; locates (and thus corrects) single-bit errors at the row–column intersection.
  12. Checksum = 1’s complement addition; receiver accepts when the complemented total is all zeros.
  13. CRC = modulo-2 division by the generator; remainder 0 at the receiver ⇒ error-free; good generators have MSB and LSB = 1 (e.g. 1011 = x³+x+1).
  14. Redundancy types: data, path (network), hardware, software — buy reliability at the price of cost and complexity.
  15. IEEE 802 covers OSI Layers 1–2: 802.1 management/bridging · 802.2 LLC · 802.3 Ethernet · 802.4 Token Bus · 802.5 Token Ring · 802.11 Wi-Fi.
  16. 802.3 access = CSMA/CD; 802.11 = CSMA/CA; 802.4/802.5 = token passing.
  17. MAC sublayer = framing + 48-bit addressing + CRC/FCS error detection + medium access; implemented in the NIC.
  18. Topologies: mesh n(n−1)/2 links · star (central device) · bus (broadcast backbone) · ring (circular, hop-to-hop).

tap to flip
answer

question

The bandwidth–baud–levels formula?

tap to flip
answer

Bandwidth (bps) = Baud rate × log₂(L), L = number of signal levels. 1200 baud, L = 16 → 4800 bps.

question

Why is a single-bit error dangerous despite being “small”?

tap to flip
answer

One flip changes the decoded value entirely — 0111 (7) becomes 0011 (3) — and the damage propagates through all later logic.

question

Define burst error and its causes.

tap to flip
answer

Multiple bits corrupted within a very short time span (usually adjacent) — caused by impulsive noise / line interference; fought with Reed–Solomon or convolutional codes.

question

VRC’s blind spot?

tap to flip
answer

Any even number of bit errors — the parity stays even, so 1010101 → 1111101 passes the check undetected.

question

What can 2-D parity (LRC + VRC) do that VRC alone cannot?

tap to flip
answer

Detect and correct single-bit errors (row × column intersection pinpoints the bit) and detect 2–3 bit errors anywhere in the matrix.

question

Checksum: what arithmetic, and when does the receiver accept?

tap to flip
answer

1’s complement addition (end-around carry). Accept when the complement of the sum of all received segments = all zeros.

question

CRC: the basis, the CRC size, and the codeword length?

tap to flip
answer

Modulo-2 (XOR) binary division; CRC = k−1 bits; codeword = n + k − 1 bits. Receiver’s remainder 0 ⇒ accept.

question

Match: 802.3, 802.4, 802.5 — Ethernet / Token Bus / Token Ring?

tap to flip
answer

802.3 = Ethernet (CSMA/CD) · 802.4 = Token Bus (factory / MAP) · 802.5 = Token Ring (IBM).

question

Structure of a MAC address?

tap to flip
answer

48 bits — first 24 bits = OUI (manufacturer), last 24 bits = device-specific; burned into the NIC. E.g. 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E.

question

tap to flip
answer

n(n−1)/2 links, each node needs n−1 interfaces. n = 5 → 10 links, 4 ports each.
